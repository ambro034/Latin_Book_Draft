# Introduction and note to instructors

This chapter explains the goals, trajectory, and foundational material for the textbook, and it is integral to the approach for students and instructors.

Purpose:

This book is the result of a project to modernize the way Latin is presented in the classroom. Its mission is to retain and rely upon the virtues of a grammar-based education model supplemented by the reading-based model and the strengths of modern language textbooks. The book is thereby based on the following basic principles:

-   Pattern recognition— A major strength of modern language textbooks is their acknowledgement that many students do not start the semester with a usable knowledge of grammar (English or Latin). The typical approach for ancient language instruction is to teach the grammatical labels and terms first. In this book the approach is different and more usefull for students. With each new concept, we do not focus on labels and technical terminology until the concept is introduced and reinforced. The primary focus is pattern recognition. Most students can identify the feeling of walking into a room, seeing certain characteristics and décor of that room, and then knowing what type of situation they are walking into (dorm, study group, basketball tournament watching party, etc.). The aim of this book is to help instructors and students teach the same recognition with Latin sentences: is this a transitive or intransitive sentence? Is this a sentence that requires a special verb? At the beginning the goal is pattern recognition at the expense of colloquial speech. Over time, students will learn the patterns of grammar and vocabulary, and therefore be able to read any sentence pattern with just a little vocabulary help. This inverts the old approach of building vocabulary and terminology first, which can prove frustrating for the instructor and student. The reward for pattern recognition pays off with the structure of each chapter.
-   Anchoring— One of the biggest challenges in Latin is the flood of new material. Students are asked to learn a new syntax, new vocabulary, and a new structure all at once. The second major approach of this book is to anchor students in what they know before moving on to new information. For that reason, some information will be presented in a different order than standard grammatical textbooks. Core grammatical ideas like dependent clauses, passive voice, participles, and use of infinitives are introduced early so students will internalize fundamental grammatical principles, then they will have them as anchors for new concepts. For example, the future tense, although similar in form to present and imperfect, is introduced much later than usual, since it is rare in most Latin texts and does not anchor any additional concepts.
-   Statistical and semantic approach to vocabulary— Only 30 of the 500 most common words in classical Latin are first declension nouns, and 48 are from the second declension. Conversely 87 are from the third. It does not make sense then to teach the first declension paradigm early on, when through the 3rd declension students can learn about noun gender, stem changes, and more common vocabulary. Similar distinctions can be found in the verb conjugations: 1st conjugation is just not common enough to be introduced first. This book introduced the first 3 declensions and first 2 conjugations together. Although fourth and fifth declensions are not introduced until the second semester, all conjugations are rapidly introduced. This book also groups particular families of vocabulary terms (earth/land terms; government; household; etc), in a way similar to what most modern language books use.
-   Statistical approach to grammar— Cases are introduced in order of importance, and importance equates to frequency. Many grammatical ideas will be delayed or omitted if they are not statistically relevant. This allows the case use to become an anchor before the paradigm is memorized. Similar approaches are n with verbs and adjectives.
-   Sentences and stories— Some of the practice sentences, especially in the first few chapters and in chapter exercises, are unrelated and designed to help introduce grammatical points. After chapter 3, short passages adapted from Latin texts or stories will reinforce previous concepts and anticipate future ones.
-   This book is written with the idea that although many students do not plan to become classics majors, all students do need a more nuanced approach to grammar, vocabulary, and communication in general. Significant literary, cultural, and historical information is therefore put aside or placed in an appendix so the student can focus on the primary goal of pattern recognition and language development. In classroom testing, chapters 1-10 are introduced in the first semester, chapters 11-19 in the second, and chapters 20 through the end are included for a possible third semester.  
    

About Latin

Learning Latin is like learning to restore an old car that’s sitting in your garage. Although you may never use it daily, the lessons you learn will help you with future repairs and improvements that are more urgent. If you learn about the ancestors of our ancestors’ culture by understanding it, tinkering with it, and exploring, it will make other languages, communication in general, cultural interaction, and history come alive in new ways.

# Pronunciation and Fundamental Grammar

## The basics of pronunciation

Languages and alphabets are not the same thing. An alphabet represents a language, but one alphabet can represent different languages (French and English), or one language can even have different alphabets (Cantonese and Mandarin). The alphabet that we use in English is called the Latin alphabet, because it is (almost) the same as the alphabet the Romans used. There are only a few minor differences:

-   The Romans did not have a ‘J’ in their alphabet. They had an ‘I’ that sometimes worked as a vowel, sometimes as a consonant. The ‘I’ that worked as a consonant became a ‘J’. In ancient Latin, it was pronounced like the Y in “yes.” Remembering the difference in your vocabulary can be difficult, but practice and common sense will help. For example, in the Latin word *iudex*, which means *judge*, the ‘i’ is a consonant (hence the ‘j’ in judge). It would be pronounced *you-dex*, whereas *insula* (whence the English word *peninsula*) is pronounced *in-sū-la*.
-   The Romans did not have a ‘W’. At different points in history, the ‘U’ and the ‘V’ were written as the same letter. The ‘U’ and ‘V’ could be pronounced like either the ‘U’ in “campus” (which is actually a Latin word) or like the ‘W’ in “wind.”
-   In Classical Latin, the letters ‘C’ and ‘G’ were always hard. ‘C’ was pronounced like the ‘C’ in “curse,” never like the ‘C’ in “cent” or the ‘ch’ in “cheese.” ‘G’ was always pronounced like ‘G’ in “guppy,” but never like the ‘G’ in “gentle.”
-   Vowels are always pronounced. Pay close attention to long vowels, marked by a **macron**, especially at the end of words. There is some variation about how to pronounce vowels, and Latin certainly had variation over its geographic and temporal realm. In general, use the following guidelines for Classical Latin
    -   a: like the first syllable in *aware*; ā: like “open up your mouth and say *ahhh*”
    -   e: like the e in *temporary*; ē: like the a in *lake*
    -   i: like the i in *it* (unless it is a consonant); ī: like the vowel sound in *beak*
    -   o: like the o in *doctor*; ō: like the o in *bingo*
    -   u: like the u in *us*; ū: like oo sound in *doom*
    -   Diphthongs: often 2 vowels combine into 1 sound. English does this with words like *school*, *break*, and *guile*. There are several, but the most common are:
        -   ae: like the ‘I’ in *right*
        -   ai: like the ‘AY’ in *pay*
        -   oe: like the ‘OI’ in *boing*
        -   au: like the ‘AU’ in *audio*

## The Basics of Language

Many students have heard the (false) rumor that word order did not matter in Latin. The rules of word order were indeed much more flexible than those of English, but patterns still defined the rules of language.  

Latin, like most early languages (including the linguistic ancestor to English) was inflected; the endings of a word—not its position in the sentence—tell you the word’s job in the sentence. English has traces of its inflection in words like “he,” “him,” and “his.” It would be incorrect to say “Him sees she,” even though the words are correct, because the endings are wrong. It is important that you pay attention to the endings of every word you read in Latin.  

Latin was centered around clauses. A clause in linguistic terms is anything with a **subject** and a **predicate** (“she is,” “they walk,” or “Dead men tell no tales”) are all clauses. Independent clauses are like the previous examples: they do not have to be a sentence, but they do have to stand by themselves (“he walks,” not “if he walks…”). Dependent clauses (“once we get there,” “however much you plan”) still have subjects and predicates, but they need to be joined to an independent clause. In English we tend to write with lots of independent clauses, but the Romans typically had one or two independent clauses with many dependent clauses. Therefore even the longest Latin sentences can often be read by just noting individual clauses and their relationship to each other.

# Word Endings

### Principles of grammar

Today, words that change forms (he brings, he brought; child, children) are thought to be irregular, but most languages (including English), began with lots of different endings for all words. Speakers changed these endings according to the role each word pays in the sentence. Most modern languages instead use word order to express this information. This is called **inflection** (from the Latin word for *flex* or *bend*, because one can bend the words in different ways to mean something different).

Consider the phrase “The friends meet the players.”

You already read by patterns, but English patterns rely more on word order than Latin. We know who (friends) is doing something (drink) and what they are doing (coffee). We also know that the friends are mine (and not the coffee) and that the coffee is hot (and not necessarily my friends). In many ancient languages, though, speakers would express this information through word endings instead.

We could create a language system in which endings of words tell us what the word is doing, so if:

-   ! means “performs the action” (i.e., Subject)
-   \# means “receives the action” (i.e., Direct Object)

Then we could move the words around to emphasize different parts of the sentence:

-   Meet friends! players# “The friends meet the players.”
-   Friends! players# meet “The friends meet the players.”
-   Friends# players! meet “The players meet the friends.”
-   Players! friends# meet “The players meet the friends.”

We’ll see that the Romans used endings not only to show what a noun is doing in a sentence, but number (one friend or many friends), but what verbs are doing as well. **When** the friends and players meet, **if** they meet, and more. All of this is told with endings, so they are essential to memorize.

The most important task you have in starting Latin is to identify this new pattern of endings instead of word order. This is like driving in a new country. Lots of the rules are still the same, but there are important differences (which side of the road should you drive on?).

-   Latin uses inflections, but instead of exclamation marks or pound signs, the Romans just used different letters at the end of words.
-   For now, we will focus on 2 types of inflected words (nouns and verbs).

Transitive/Intransitive

In active voice sentences, words that perform the action are called **subjects**. Words that receive that action are called **direct objects**.

In her book *Word by Word*, Kory Stamper describes the sophisticated method by which editors of the *Merriam Webster Dictionary* determine how transitive a verb is. They have a notecard with the words “I'ma \_\_\_\_ yo ass!” written on it (with a hole cut out for the verb in question). If the editor puts the card over a verb and the sentence makes grammatical sense, then it’s transitive.

-   try with verbs like SNEEZE, MOVE, REALIZE, CALL, CONSIDER, SLEEP. Which ones are transitive?
-   Another simple way to test if a verb is transitive is to see if what? or whom? work with i.
    -   “they see” what? (a movie)
    -   “he knows” whom? (the plot)
    -   This test won’t work with “she stares” what?

### Principles of Latin

##### Verbs

There are (for now) 2 families (called **conjugations**) of verbs: a verbs and e verbs. There is not a significant difference to these verbs; they just belong to different families and have slight spelling variations.

For these verbs (and almost all verbs), t signifies “he/she/it \_\_\_\_\_s” and nt signifies “they \_\_\_\_\_”. Unlike English, Latin, like many other languages, does not require a named subject. When we translate, we should supply an appropriate: *vocant* = *they call*.

This form (called the **present tense**) can be translated in multiple ways in English. The most common are

-   simple present: *they call*
-   emphatic present: *they do call*
-   progressive present: *they are calling*

We’ll visit these concepts more in chapter 5, but for now just remember that they are all good ways to translate.

vocat/vocant: he/she/it calls; they call

videt/vident: he/she/it sees; they see

habet/habent: he/she/it has; they have

vocat/vocant: he/she/it calls; they call

movet/movent: he/she/it moves; they move

est/sunt: he/she/it is; they are (This is an irregular, but very common verb. It does not belong to any verb family, but it still uses the same endings.)

##### Nouns

There are several different families (called **declensions**) of nouns. These also have spelling variations that will be covered in depth later. The ending of a noun signifies its function in the sentence. In the following list, nouns ending in **m** (singular) or **s** (plural) are direct objects of sentences.

For now, any noun that doesn’t end in **m** or **s** are subjects.

pater videt pueru**m**: the father sees the boy

pater pueru**m** videt: the father sees the boy

videt pater pueru**m**: the father sees the boy

pater videt puer**os**: the father sees the boys

patrēs vident pueru**m**: **the fathers** see the boy

puerī patrem vident: **the boys** see the father

-   In the last two sentences, the subjects were plural.
-   Even if you don’t know all the singular or plural endings, you can tell its number by the verb ending (-t/-nt).

##### Vocabulary memorization

When you learn a noun, there are 4 essential elements of each word you need to memorize. You’ll learn more about the terms later:

| puer—_nominative_ _singular_ | puerī—_genitive_ _singular_ | masculine—_gender_ | boy—_definition_ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

1.  *Nominative* *singular*: this is essentially where you’d look in a dictionary to find the word. It is also the **subject** form.
2.  *Genitive* *singular*: this is more important than the first: it tells you the noun’s *stem* (the form on which all other versions of the word will be based) and its family (which determines how the word will look as a subject, direct object, etc.)
3.  *Gender*: all Latin nouns belong to one of 3 genders: masculine; feminine; neuter. You CANNOT determine a noun’s gender by its ending, definition, appearance, or anything else. It must be memorized.
4.  *Definition*: remember that words change meaning all the time, so even though an English word may look like its Latin root, it won’t always mean the same thing. *Manufacture* comes from the Latin words *manus, manūs (f) hand* and *faciō, facere, fēcī, factus: to make*, but it means the opposite of “to make by hand.”

##### Noun Forms

Remember that you’ll learn memorize two forms of each noun. The first (nominative) tells you the dictionary entry; the second (genitive) tells you the noun’s family and its stem.

-   When the second form ends with an -ae (see chapter 3), it belongs to the **first declension**
-   When the second form ends with an -ī, it belongs to the **second declension**
-   When the second form ends with an -is, it belongs to **the third declension**

To find the noun’s stem (you’ll put all the endings you learn in these chapters on the stem), remove the ending from the second form.

locī — -ī = *loc* (stem)

patris — -is = *patr* (stem)

### Pattern Recognition

When memorizing a word, it’s important to remember not just what it means in English, but what it does in a sentence. Pay close attention to if a noun is a subject or object, or singular or plural. Decide if a verb is transitive or intransitive before you move to the rest of the sentence. If you have an intransitive verb, you don’t need to look for a direct object.

Adjectives

Just like there are families of nouns (five), there are also families of adjectives (2). More about those families will be introduced later, but for now there is one major rule that governs adjectives and the nouns they modify, regardless of their family:

\*\*an adjective must match the case, gender, and number of the noun it modifies. Unlike nouns (which do not change gender), endings on adjectives will change to match the gender of a noun.

Remember our sentence from the previous chapter: “The friends meet the players.”

We’ll describe the players: “The friends meet the new players.”

Adjectives that describe the friends and the players show agreement by word order in English. We know the players are new (not the friends) because the words are next to each other.

In Latin, adjectives show agreement (case, gender, and number) through their endings. In our system:

-   ! means “performs the action” (i.e., Subject)
-   \# means “receives the action” (i.e., Direct Object)

Then we could move the words around to emphasize different parts of the sentence:

-   Friends! players# good# meet “The friends meet the good players.”
-   Meet friends! good# players# “The friends meet the good players.”
-   Meet friends# good! players! “The good players meet the friends.”
-   Meet friends! good! players# “The good friends meet the players.”

In the final sentence, the ending of “good” is the same as friends, so the meaning of the sentence changed.

# Linking Verbs and the Ablative Case

### Principles of Grammar

Pattern recognition: you have seen how word order in Latin is flexible; the endings of a word, not its position in the sentence, tell you its function (this is called **inflection**). You have learned two of these functions, and from now on we’ll also refer to them by their technical names: subject (**Nominative case**); and direct object (**Accusative case**).

In this chapter, we’ll begin to learn other functions. We’ll also discuss a new sentence pattern—**linking**.

Linking Verbs In addition to transitive (can take a direct object) verbs and intransitive (cannot take a direct object) verbs, linking verbs are widely used in Latin (and most languages). A linking verb does just what it says: it links a subject to a predicate. The most common linking verb in Latin and English is a form of “to be.” There is no direct object because no action is performed. Linking verbs do, however, take a **predicate nominative**. This is just a noun or adjective that is not the subject, but it is in the nominative (e.g., “the book is *long*,” or “the boat is a *yacht*”).

In Latin, the two linking verbs for this chapter are:

-   est = he/she/it is
-   sunt = they are

Prepositions these words typically identify relationships (mainly involving time or space) between words or clauses. For example, someone can come *before* the fourth day (time) or stand *before* the building (space). *Place* prepositions are far more common than *time* prepositions, so you’ll learn those first.

Although it doesn’t fit every preposition, there is an easy test to determine the types of words that are prepositions. Just plug them into the famous nursery rhyme: Hickory, dickory, dock. The mouse ran \_\_\_\_ the clock.

Declensions

You’ll note that different words have different sets of endings (for example, *puella*, *puellam*, and *puellā* all have a, but *mens*, *mentem*, and *mente* do not). They belong to different families of words. These families are called **declensions**. A declension is just a category that lets you memorize endings and find patterns more easily. There are five of these declensions, but you’ll only focus on three for a while.

### Principles of Latin

##### Neuter Nouns

The previous chapter introduced neuter adjective endings. In this chapter, you will learn some neuter nouns. As with any other noun, the word’s definition does not determine its gender; it is just part of the word’s meaning. With all neuter nouns, no matter the case, the nominative (subject case) and accusative (object case) always have the same endings. Context will help you determine its use in the sentence.

##### Location and motion Away (Ablative case)

So far you have seen two case uses of Latin nouns: subjects and direct objects. In this chapter, you will learn a new case and use—place. A more complete layout of these functions and their system will begin in chapter 7, but for now you can just familiarize yourself with the ending. The name applied to words with these endings is the **Ablative Case**

-   \-ā, -ō, -e (singular),
-   \-īs, -ibus (plural)

If a noun has one of these endings it will indicate either: 1) motion away from something; or 2) location (in or on something).

Examples:

-   They jumped *down from* the last step (motion away)
-   They ran *away from* the steps (motion away).
-   They remained *on* the last step (location).

In Latin, as in English, these spatial relationships typically follow the same patterns that are determined by prepositions. For example, the boy (*puer*) can stand in the field (*in agrō*) or walk away from the field (*de agrō*). In both examples, the ō at the end of the word shows that the word *field* in this category (not the subject or object category), and the preposition shows the exact relationship. Also like English, Latin prepositions often do a lot of heavy lifting, so one preposition can show several different relationships.

\***An important early tool for translation: remember that prepositions show relationships to words, and those relationships are typically different in every language**. Therefore, there is not often a 1:1 correspondence in translation. Sometimes the Latin preposition *in* can be translated the same as English (in), but in can also mean into, onto, against, among (and more). Learn what the words are doing in the sentence (this is called **syntax**), not simply what they would look like in English.

# All about verbs – Part 1

### Principles of Grammar

You have practiced Latin using 3^rd^ person subjects like he, she, it, and they. There are other types of subjects, and many other characteristics of verbs. These characteristics are called **attributes**. Attributes include information like when an action occurred (**tense**), how definite the speaker is about the action (**mood**), who or what the subject is (**person**), and how many subjects there are (**number**).

In English, we sometimes communicate through inflection (changing the end of the verb) I *walk* / he *walks*; we sometimes change the sound/spelling of a verb—I *run* / I *ran*—and sometimes we use helping verbs—I *walk* / I *was* *walking*. In Latin, inflection (along with occasional spelling changes, to be discussed later) is responsible for all these changes in attributes. The first two of the five attributes of Latin verbs are in the Principles of Latin section below.

Different endings of the verb show a difference in person, number, and tense. Verbs also have a form that is “without endings,” the **infinitive** (*in* = not ; *fin* = end). In English we express the infinitive of a verb as “*to \_\_\_\_.”* This form can be used in a variety of ways: I want to talk with you; I want you to talk with him; To err is human; I have come to tell you something. In coming chapters, you will learn the many ways that the Latin infinitive works (sometimes in ways similar to English, sometimes not).

### Principles of Latin

Attributes of verbs: person and number These attributes should be memorized in order. Memorizing the sequence and repeating it with every verb will allow you to instantly see all the information a verb communicates. Three other aspects (tense, voice, and mood) will be introduced later.

-   Person: Like English, Latin has three persons: 1^st^ (I, we); 2^nd^ (you, y’all); and 3^rd^ (he/she/it, they). You have been using third person, and now you will learn the other two in this chapter.
-   Number: Again, just like English, Latin has two numbers: singular (1) and plural (more than 1). ^[\[1\]](#footnote-1)^

Just as *–t* or *–nt* endings denote a 3^rd^ person verb, other endings dictate the person and number of (almost) all verbs. Third person is used more often in written texts (which are all we have left of Latin), but the 1^st^ and 2^nd^ person would have been used more frequently in spoken language. In Roman comedies the forms appear everywhere.

These endings appear repeatedly through Latin in different tenses, moods, irregular verbs, and just about anywhere you can think of. It is vital to your success in Latin that you remember the personal endings.

##### Personal Endings

|  | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | -o, -m | -mus |
| 2nd | -s | -tis |
| 3rd | -t | -nt |

Because these endings apply to almost every type of verb in Latin (tenses, moods, etc.), ***it will save you a lot of time in the future if you commit them to memory right now***. If you had to look at your notes every time you saw the word “they” or “we,” you would have a hard time communicating, and the same is true of personal endings in Latin.

##### Infinitives

Latin verbs, like English verbs, have an infinitive form that does not have any personal endings. This form is given in Latin dictionaries and is important to remember when you learn vocabulary.  

Almost all infinitives end with the letters *–re*. From the infinitive form, you learn the verb’s meaning(s) and the verb’s family. The infinitives of all the -a verbs we have seen so far end with **\-āre**, and all the -verbs end with **\-ēre**. There are other families of verbs, some have **–ere** (the long and short *e* make a big difference, so memorize them for their infinitives, and some have **īre**. These verb families are called **conjugations** (related to the Latin noun *coniunx* and the part of speech *conjunction* learned earlier).

A verb’s conjugation is simply part of its vocabulary. Like declensions, conjugations are just a category of similar words. Just as some English verbs follow a pattern (swim/swam/swum, ring/rang/rung, etc.), most Latin verbs fall into a pattern that can be identified by the verb’s conjugation. Different conjugations have slightly different rules (they do all have the same personal endings!), but it’s easier to memorize a few patterns than to remember every single form of every Latin verb. For the verbs we’ve learned so far, the rules are very similar.

To add a personal ending on a verb, you should first find its **present stem**. To do this, remove the *–re* from the infinitive. The remaining stem will end with a thematic vowel, which tells you the verb’s conjugation:

-   1st conjugation: amāre - re = amā
-   2nd conjugation: monēre - re = monē
-   3rd conjugation: regere - re = rege
-   3^rd^ –io conjugation: capere - re = cape
    -   this conjugation will be fully introduced in a later chapter
-   4th conjugation: audīre – re = audī

Once you have the present stem, you can add personal endings and have a finite verb (a verb with an ending). There are a few places where the rules change slightly, but you’ll pick up on those as we go. Spelling reflects pronunciation, so these changes give linguists clues about how the Romans pronounced these words.  

##### Notes on spelling

-   In the 1st and 3rd conjugations, the thematic vowel combines with the -ō to make one syllable (so vocō and regō, not amao and regeo)
-   In the 3rd conjugation, the short e is also changed. To a Roman, regis (you rule) was faster and easier to say than reges. Because of that, the e is shortened into an i.
-   The Romans tended to avoid the sound of -int at the end of a word. Therefore 3rd conjugation verbs end with -unt (not -int) and 4th conjugation verbs end with -iunt.

# All about verbs (part 2)

### Principles of Grammar

Attributes of verbs: **Tense**. Here is where English and Latin are slightly different. In Latin (and in some degree English), tense involves two elements: time and **aspect**. The time is self-explanatory; it refers to when the action takes place.  
Aspect, which is often conflated with time, refers to the completion or duration of an action. English has more aspects than Latin, so it can sometimes be difficult to translate in the same sense. You’ve seen that in English one can say “I think,” “I am thinking,” and “I do think.” All of these are happening in the present, but they have slightly different meanings. Similarly, one can say: “I was thinking,” “I did think,” “I thought,” “I used to think,” and even “I have been thinking.” All the verbs happened in the past, but they all have different aspects.  
Latin essentially has two aspects. For now, we’ll call them *simple* and *finished*.

-   Simple tenses
    -   **present** – ongoing (I work, I do work, I am working)
    -   **imperfect** – past ongoing (I was working, I used to work, I kept working)
    -   **future** – later action (I will work) \*to be learned in chapter 15
-   Finished tenses
    -   **perfect** – complete, either in past or present\* (I worked, I have worked, I did work)
    -   **pluperfect** – prior complete action (I had worked): to be learned in chapter 15
    -   **future** **perfect** – subsequent complete action (I will have worked): to be learned in chapter 15

\*Although it seems counterintuitive to describe a present complete action, think about the phrase “I have cleaned all day, and now you’re making a mess.” Even though the action (*I have cleaned*) is complete, the importance of the action is in the present.

### Principles of Latin

##### Imperfect Tense

In the simple tenses, changes are made by adding a tense marker between the verb and its ending. For the most part, the word is formed in this way:

VERB STEM + tense marker + personal endings

For 1st and 2nd^[\[2\]](#footnote-2)^ conjugation verbs, it looks like this:

1^st^ conjugation:

VOCĀ + -ba- + m = vocābam

VOCĀ + -bā- + s = vocābās

VOCĀ + -ba- + t = vocābat

VOCĀ + -bā- + mus = vocābāmus

VOCĀ + -bā- + tis = vocābātis

VOCĀ + -ba- + nt = vocābant

2^nd^ conjugation:

VIDĒ + -ba- + m = vidēbam

VIDĒ + -ba- + s = vidēbās

VIDĒ + -ba- + t = vidēbat

VIDĒ + -ba- + mus = vidēbāmus

VIDĒ + -ba- + tis = vidēbātis

VIDĒ + -ba- + nt = vidēbant

3^rd^ and 4^th^ conjugation are slightly different.

-   For 3^rd^, the thematic vowel (short *e*) is lengthened before adding the endings. This is unlike the formation of the present tense, when the vowel was shortened to an *i*.
-   For 4^th^, a long *e* is added, but the thematic *i* remains (all forms of the 4^th^ conjugation must have an *i* in the simple tenses (present, imperfect, and future).

3^rd^ conjugation:

REGĒ + -ba- + m = regēbam

REGĒ + -ba- + s = regēbās

REGĒ + -ba- + t = regēbat

REGĒ + -ba- + mus = regēbāmus

REGĒ + -ba- + tis = regēbātis

REGĒ + -ba- + nt = regēbant

4^th^ conjugation

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + m = audiēbam

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + s = audiēbās

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + t = audiēbat

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + mus = audiēbāmus

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + tis = audiēbātis

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + nt = audiēbant

##### Perfect Tense

The perfect tense has slightly different personal endings, but the good news is that they apply to every single verb in Latin, even the most irregular ones.

To form the perfect tense, take the 3rd principal part and remove the final ī. This leaves you with the **perfect active stem**. To this stem, simply add the personal endings:

| -ī | -īmus |
| --- | --- |
| -istī | -istis |
| -it | \-ērunt/ēre[\[3\]](#footnote-3) |

So no matter what conjugation the verb is, it will adopt this paradigm:

| vocav**ī** | vocav**īmus** |
| --- | --- |
| vocav**istī** | vocav**istis** |
| vocav**it** | vocav**ērun**t / vocav**ēre** |

Depending on the aspect you want to get across in English, you can translate the perfect tense in a variety of ways: they called, they did call, they have called.

##### Forms of Sum, esse, fuī

The linking verb (sum, esse, fui, futurus) can also be conjugated in the present, imperfect, and perfect. These forms appear everywhere and are best memorized. They do follow the typical pattern of personal endings but seem slightly irregular. For those interested, this type of irregular verb is formed through **suppletion**, and it occurs in most languages (including English).

| Present |  | Imperfect |  | Perfect |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| sum | sumus | eram | eramus | fuī | fuimus |
| es | estis | eras | eratis | fuistī | fuistis |
| est | sunt | erat | erant | fuit | fuērunt/ēre |

This is another area in which Latin and English do not match up as well. Both the imperfect and perfect are usually best translated as “they were,” but you can distinguish between the imperfect (“they used to be”) and perfect (“they have been”) if necessary.

# Cases—Nouns and Pronouns

In addition to verb conjugations, Latin nouns and adjectives have families called **declensions**. Also like verb conjugations, a noun’s declension has no bearing on its gender or meaning. The 5 declensions are simply groups, and words typically—but not always—follow predictable patterns within those groups.

Principles of Grammar: Many of today’s languages (including English) began as an inflected language based on case endings. Although word order has replaced inflection, we still see remnants in how different words become plural (foot/feet, child/children, table/tables) and how we use pronouns to show possession or use in a phrase (they/them/their). For example, “them see us” would be incorrect because “them” is an object based on its ending.

Pronouns: You have seen some Latin pronouns (is/eum, ea/eam) already in this book. **Pronouns** are deceptive because native speakers tend to take them for granted; they simply replace nouns, but how they work may vary in different languages. Therefore you are encouraged to spend extra time and effort memorizing **ALL** these forms. If you had to pause and think about every pronoun you encounter or keep a pronoun dictionary handy to distinguish between “they” and “them,” it would be difficult to interact with anyone.

There are two significant differences between Latin and English pronouns.

1) because all nouns have grammatical gender in Latin, objects are referred to by their grammatical gender. If, for example, you are referring to a table (mensa, mensae, feminine), the Romans would call it *ea* or *eam* (she, her), even though we would say *it*.

2) Romans had many more third person pronouns than we do. English speakers refer to *this* (something near) or *that* (something further away), but the Romans had four different pronouns to use.

Principles of Latin: Rather than worrying about how each pronoun gets translated (because sometimes the translation changes based on context), think about the relationship between all four pronouns. They can be visualized as concentric circles:

The smaller circle (*is, ea, id*) just represents the simplest of pronouns, like “he” or “him” or “it.” The next circle (*hic, haec, hoc*) can still mean “he,” him,” or “it,” but it is sometimes more emphatic, like “this,” or “these.” *Ille, illa, illud* is more emphatic still, and can sometimes connote distance like “that” and “those.” The largest circle (*iste, ista, istud*) is less common, and often connotes separation. In legal speeches, for example, it is usually used by one party (whose client might be referred to as *is* or *hic*) when referring to the other (obviously guilty) party—*iste*.

When you encounter these pronouns, then, know that they can all refer to a he, she, it, or they, but each one has a slightly different force.

Demonstrative Adjectives: Just like in English, many pronouns can also serve as adjectives. One might say “I see that,” or “I see that table.” Each of the pronouns you have learned in Latin can likewise appear as a demonstrative adjective. Like any adjective, it must agree with the noun it modifies in case, number, and gender.

Cases: You have already learned the subject case (nominative), object case (accusative), and a directional case (ablative). In this chapter you’ll learn the last two major cases. In coming chapters, you’ll see how all the cases can be used in multiple ways (not just for subjects and objects).

### Principles of Latin

Latin has 7 cases, but 5 are far more common (the last two will be introduced later):

| Nominative (names the noun) | Subject – the _chef_ cooks   Predicate Nominative – she is a _chef_ |
| --- | --- |
| Genitive (describes, classifies, or restricts the noun) | Possession – the _chef’s_ hat   Partitive – some o_f the chefs_   Description – a chef _of great fame_ |
| Dative (describes who/what benefits from an action or who/what the action affects) | Indirect object – give the book t_o the chef\*_   Interest – we cook _for the chef_ |
| Accusative (describes the goal, direction, or extent of a verb; indicates direct object of transitive verb; shows a length of time) | Direct object – We see the _chef_   Motion toward – Take this t_o the chef\*_   Duration – The chef cooks _for many hours_ |
| Ablative (describes motion from, location, and instrument, shows a specific time) | Motion – Move the knives _away from the chef_   Place – There is soup _on the chef_   Instrument – the chef chops _with his knife_   Specific time – the chef is busy _at this time_. |

\*Both the dative and accusative can sometimes be translated as *to the chef*. The Romans tended to prefer the accusative if movement were more important and dative if benefit were more important, but these occasionally overlapped. For example, in the phrase “Caesar sends the reinforcements to the battle,” both the movement and benefit are important, but the Romans would tend to use the accusative to show motion. Be flexible in translation.

This is not an exhaustive list; it just covers the basics. Every declension, however, has the same cases and case uses. So once you learn the cases, you’ll be on the way to identifying all possible uses of nouns and pronouns.

Note that you cannot determine a word’s declension by its nominative form. To do that, you must memorize the genitive singular. The ending of this word (-ae, ī, or -is) tells you the declension (1st, 2nd, or 3rd, respectively). The part of the word that remains after you identify the ending is called the **Noun Stem**.

For the noun:

puer, puerī (m): boy

-   Observe the genitive singular (always the second form in vocabulary): *puerī*
-   Identify the word as second declension *\-ī*
-   Note the noun stem: *puer-*

You must use the stem to form the inflected forms of the noun. This doesn’t make a big difference in some words (like puella/puellae, or regnum/regnī), but it does in most words (like ager/agrī, mēns/mentis, and litus/litoris).

A few other important details about the declensions:

-   Endings for masculine and feminine nouns of the same declension are typically the same.
-   Most first declension nouns are feminine and most second declension nouns are not (this isn’t a rule, though).
-   There are no 1st declension neuter nouns.
-   The only significant difference between M/F nouns and N nouns is the neuter law: all nominative and accusatives are the same if the word is neuter.

Pattern Recognition

-   Demonstratives can be pronouns (he, she, him, they, them, etc.) or adjectives (*this* person, *those* mountains, *that* guy). If the word is close to another word of the same gender, case, and number, it is likely and adjective. If it’s a pronoun, always identify the antecedent.
-   Antecedents typically come before the pronoun, but they do not have to. Compare the English sentence: We asked for her, but the manager was out of her office.

# Numbers, time, and distance

### Principles of Grammar

Generally, the accusative case shows measures of space and intervals of time, while ablative shows specifics of location and instances of time. For example, if someone walks for 2 miles over 2 hours, both of those would be represented in accusative. If someone arrived at the 2^nd^ mile after the 2^nd^ hour, the Romans would express that with an ablative.

As for numbers, the Romans used (wait for it…) Roman numerals. For the purposes of this book, you only need to learn the basics. That includes the numbers one through ten thousand in **numerals**, one through ten in the **cardinal** and first through tenth in **ordinal**.

### Principles of Latin

##### 3^rd^ i-stem nouns

Some Latin nouns come from an older i-based declension, but they were absorbed by the more common family of the 3^rd^ declension. There are just a few differences between these nouns and regular 3^rd^ declension nouns: the Ablative singular is often –ī instead of –e; the genitive plural is –ium instead of –um; and neuter plurals (nominative and accusative) end in –ia, not –a.

There are 3 ways a noun can be a 3^rd^ i-stem. Memorize these categories as required, but always be ready to recognize them when reading.

1.  The noun is masculine or feminine and the stem (genitive singular) ends in two consonants (urbs, urbis (f); city)
2.  The noun is masculine or feminine and the nominative and genitive are parisyllabic (they have the same number of syllables—e.g., nubes, nubis (f); cloud)
3.  The noun is neuter and the nominative ends in –al (e.g., animal, animalis (n): animal), -ar (e.g., exemplar, exemplaris (n): example), or –e (e.g., mare, maris (n): sea)

##### 3^rd^ Declension adjectives

3^rd^ declension i-stem nouns provide the endings for a far more common family of words, 3^rd^ declension adjectives. You can survive most Latin just knowing that both types of 3^rd^ declension nouns exist, but you will need to know the endings for adjectives (see the *Principles of Latin*, Chapter 13).

##### Roman Numerals

The main basis for Roman numeral is the decimal system, from the Latin term *decem* (10). The primary numerals are:

-   I = one
-   X = ten
-   C = one hundred
-   M = one thousand

Each number is 10x the previous. For numbers between 1 and 10 or 10 and 100, numbers from one decimal position away can be added or subtracted. Smaller numbers after one of these numbers add; smaller numbers before a number subtract.

For example:

-   X = 10
-   XI = 11
-   IX = 9
-   All Roman numerals (until we get to very large numbers like 10,000) are just an expansion of this concept

Of course, it becomes difficult to keep adding numbers, and four “ones” become a lot to add—IIII—especially if you carve them into stone. Therefore, Romans used intermediate numerals to help:

-   V = five
-   L = fifty
-   D = five hundred

The same rules of addition and subtraction apply.

For example:

-   XV (10 + 5) = 15
-   LXXXI (50 + 30 + 1) = 81
-   XLI (-10 + 50 + 1) = 41

The last rule is that you must remain within one decimal value of the largest number. Therefore, IC cannot be used for 99 (even though -1 + 100 = 99) because 100 and 1 are two decimal values apart. Therefore, you must use XCXIX ( -10 + 100 + -1 +10). This is confusing at first, but you will get the hang of it with practice.

Additional notes:

-   To multiply a number by 1,000, write a bar above the letter. Therefore x̄ = 10,000
-   There is no way to represent the number zero in Roman numerals

##### Accusative with time and space

The accusative can show a time before a specific event, after a certain event, or the duration of the event. To show time before (these prepositions will be listed in the vocabulary section as well), Romans used the preposition *ante*, then the noun in the accusative. For time after, they use *post*. For duration, no preposition is necessary. For example, *ante-meridiem* (AM) is “before mid-day,” and *post-meridiem* (PM) is “after mid-day.” The –em ending is the accusative form. “During these times” (tempus, temporis (n.) time) is *illa tempora* (neuter plural).

The same applies for space. One can stand in front of a temple (*ante templum*), behind a temple (*post templum*) or walk through a temple (*per templum*).

##### Ablative with time and space

The ablative typically shows the specific event or location itself. *illo tempore* = “at that time” and *in illo templo* = “in that temple.” While the accusative case shows a range of time and space, the ablative tends to show a specific time and space. Instead of “during these times,” ablative says “in this time,” “on this day,” “at this hour,” etc.

Pattern Recognition

-   The new prepositions follow the same pattern as the directional prepositions you have already learned. Once you see the preposition, keep reading until you find the correct case. Everything in between will be part of the segment. |*ante dei et deae templa* | *sedimus* = We sit | in front of the temples of the god and goddess|.

# Infinitives and Ablative Uses

### Principles of Grammar

The infinitive, or “not finite” form of the verb, is typically translated as “to \_\_\_” (*to go*, *to run*). It is the most basic form of the verb and does not have personal endings (hence its names). So far, we have just used the infinitive to find the present stem, but the infinitive itself appears in many forms. Most of those forms correspond to English uses of the infinitive (one use, the **indirect statement**, is not like English; you’ll learn that in a later chapter).

In reading chapters, you have seen several words that often go with infinitives. Words like “he is able” (*potest*) or “they want” (*volunt*) are accompanied by an infinitive, just like they are in English: “he is able *to walk*” or “they want *to dance*.”

The Ablative case, in addition to showing location and motion away, has many uses in Latin, most of them can be translated by the English preposition “with” or “from.” The two uses in this chapter can both be translated “with,” but they are very different constructions.

### Principles of Latin

  
Two uses of the infinitive correspond very closely with English. The 4th will be introduced in a later chapter. With all these uses, sentence patterns, especially a finite verb in the same clause, will help you recognize what type of infinitive you see.

infinitive with Modal Verb: an infinitive with this type of verb is also called **complementary** because its use “completes” the meaning of another verb. **Modal verbs** include *can (be able), want, wish, opt, prefer, dare*, all of which need an infinitive (“I want to walk” or “He dares to arrive late”).

  
Pattern recognition—Complementary infinitives will often appear in clauses that have verbs like *possum* (I am able), *debeo* (I ought), and *volo* (I want).  
*possumus ambulāre*: we are able to walk / we can walk  
*debent canere*: they ought to sing

*volo regere*: I want to rule

Infinitive as a Subject: The infinitive can function as a nominative, neuter, singular noun and can therefore serve as the subject. This allows the action to be described or to perform an action itself

Pattern recognition—Infinitives used as nouns will be the subject; other sentence patterns like transitive, intransitive, and linking, can still be present. There will not typically be a verb that depends on an infinitive (like *possum*), unless it is dependent on another infinitive that takes a complementary (and thus combines both patterns:  
*legere bonum est*: reading is good  
*iacēre eum servat*: lying down protects him

*legere posse bonum est*: being able to read is good

Ablative of Means/Instrument: the literal or figurative means by which something is accomplished. We write *with our pens*. We love *with all our hearts*. He defeated the enemy *with his sword*.

Pattern recognition—To express the means or instruments by which something happens, the Romans typically used the ablative **without** a preposition (means/instrument):

vidēmus oculīs: we see with our eyes

homō nōs beneficiō iūvit: the man pleased us with kindness

Ablative of Accompaniment: shows the people or animals participating in the clause. We write *with our friends*. He fought *with the enemy*.

Pattern recognition—To express the person (or animals) that participate in an action, the Romans typically used the ablative with the preposition *cum* (accompaniment):

cum multīs equīs ambulāmus: we walk with many horses

canimus cum coniugibus: we sing with (our) spouses

Other personal pronouns:

is/ea/id is the 3rd person pronoun, but Latin also has 1st and 2nd person pronouns. The nominative form of these isn’t generally necessary. *ego amō* and *amō* both mean “I love,” but the pronoun can either show emphasis or signal a change in the grammatical subject.

Personal pronouns do not decline like any other nouns, but they all follow a similar pattern:

# Imperative mood and passive voice

### Principles of Grammar

Thus far we have covered 3 of the 5 attributes of verbs: person (1st, 2nd, 3rd); number (singular, plural), and tense (present, imperfect, and perfect). In this chapter, you’ll learn about the remaining two attributes.

Mood:

The indicative, in which the speaker is just “indicating” what he/she perceives, has been the mood of all verbs thus far. The imperative mood expresses the speaker’s will for something to happen. The most common imperative form (and the only one covered in this book) is the second person imperative. Although we don’t typically say “you” in English, it is implied in a phrase like “(you) Get the book for me.” Likewise, uses of the imperative are typically present tense and **active voice**.

Voice: In grammatical terms, voice refers to the relationship between the subject and the action. In Latin and English, the **passive voice** is used when the subject receives the action. Until now, we have only used sentences in which the subject performs the action (active) or describes the predicate (linking). Transitive verbs can be active or passive; intransitive verbs are rarely passive (it is impossible to “be remained”).

Passive voice can be frustrating and difficult for students in the beginning, but it is commonly used in Latin. In fact, learning the rules in Latin often helps students with their English use. Active and passive voice clauses can contain the same information, but they emphasize different parts.

For example:

-   Active: “The students see that book.” (the subject—students—performs the action)
-   Passive: “That book is seen by the students.” (the subject—book—receives the action)

In both sentences, the information is the same, but one focuses on the students, while the other stresses the book.

English forms passive voice with the past participle of the verb and a helping verb (in the example above, *seen* is the past participle and *is* is the helping verb). The helping verb changes the tense and number of the main verb, while the participle remains the same. **Remember that the presence of a participle or helping verb does not by itself indicate passive voice**.

The door is opened by the student. (present passive)

The door was opened by the student. (past passive)

The student was opening the door. (past active)

### Principles of Latin

The present imperative is formed from the present stem. Although other forms of the imperative exist, they are not common.

To create the singular imperative, you simply use the present stem (the infinitive without the –re). To make the imperative plural, you simply need to add –te. The 3rd and 3rd -io forms are (like always) slightly different:

Singular Plural

1st conjugation vocā (you) call! vocāte (y’all) all!

2nd conjugation vidē (you) see! vidēte (y’all) see!

3rd conjugation rege (you) act! **regite** (y’all) act!

3rd -io conjugation cape (you) seize! **capite** (y’all) seize!

4th conjugation audī (you) hear! audīte (y’all) hear!

Passive voice is also simple to form in Latin. For the present and imperfect tense, you’ll continue to use the present stem (just like for active voice). The 3rd principal part forms only the perfect active stem, so now you will use the 4th principal part to form the perfect passive tense.

For the present and imperfect, all of the usual rules apply to forming the stem and the tense. Latin just has different personal endings. So instead of

\-ō/-m -mus use the passive endings: -r -mur

\-s -tis -ris -mini

\-t -nt -tur -ntur

##### Ablative of agent

This ablative use occurs with a passive voice verb to show the person or animal that performs the action. In an active voice pattern, the subject performs the action, so this case use is unique to the passive voice pattern.

Agent is like Ablative of Means, but an Agent must be a person (or personified animal). And while Ablative of Means uses no preposition, Agent uses ā/ab.

Agent: *is vincitur* ***ā Caesare***: he is conquered ***by Caesar***

Means/instrument: *is vincitur* ***armīs***: he is conquered ***with weapons***

Pattern Recognition

In English, passive verbs always have a “be” helping verb (“it is thought,” “the ball was thrown,” “we have been seen”), but that “be” verb doesn’t make something passive (“he is thinking,” “the ball was rolling,” “we have been seeing”). Always focus on what the word is doing, not just what it might look like.

In Latin, this isn’t always the case. The pattern is \*subject\* (receives) \*verb\* (optional: by person (agent) or thing (means)

# Indirect Statement and Reflexives

### Principles of Grammar:

We do not often directly quote someone else when we report what they say. Instead, we give an approximate summary. For example: “Caesar said that the soldiers wanted to depart tomorrow.” This is not a quote, just an explanation of the events. This is called indirect statement. The term statement is misleading, because other senses can be used as well. For example, a similar pattern would exist for: Caesar heard/felt/believed/remembered/claimed/decided/saw that the soldiers wanted to depart tomorrow.

There are dozens of words (in Latin and English) that can start an indirect statement, but typically they all originate in the subject’s head (mouth, ears, mind, etc.), so we’ll use the term “head verb.”

Indirect statements typically include other common elements, too. Consider another example (Gaius says that the men see the enemy) in its syntactical breakdown.

| Gaius | says | that | the men | see | the enemy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| main subject | head verb | grammatical particle | subject of indirect statement | finite verb | direct object of indirect statement |

This is the pattern for indirect statements in English. Latin indirect statements communicate the same information but use their own pattern.

| Caesar | says | that | the men | see | the enemy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gaius | dicit | &nbsp; | virōs | vidēre | hostem |
| main subject | head verb | &nbsp; | accusative subject of indirect statement | infinitive | direct object of indirect statement |

### Principles of Latin:

##### Indirect Statement

The differences between the two languages are small, but you will have to adapt the Latin pattern to English when you translate.

-   Latin does not use the particle *that*, so you must supply it in the translation. The particle is not always necessary (e.g., “we believe you are near” or “we believe that you are near”), but it helps to identify the pattern if you use it every time.
-   Latin uses an accusative subject, so you must change it to the subject in English by placing it in the right word order (subject comes first). For pronouns, accusatives should be expressed as nominatives (not *him/her/them* but *he/she/they*)
-   Latin uses an infinitive, so you must change it to a finite verb in English.

Here is another example: *Nōs putāmus eum servāre equōs* (“we think that he protects the horses”—**NOT** “we think him to protect the horses”).

Objective infinitive: This is functionally the same as an indirect statement. A group of verbs (e.g., to order, to wish) tend to follow a different pattern in English (“We order them to go,” not “We order that they go”). Although the Latin pattern is the same, the translations require English infinitives. Aside from *iubeō* (chapter 9), words that can take this pattern are the irregular verbs *volō, nolō,* and *malō* (see appendix) and the semi-deponent verb *audeō* (chapter 18).

  
Pattern recognition—Objective infinitives take the same pattern as indirect statement in Latin, but the verb indicates a slightly different English pattern.

*iubet nōs iuvāre*: he orders us to help  
*iubēmus eās movēre*: we order them to move

| We | think | that | he | protects | the horses. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nōs | putāmus | &nbsp; | eum | servāre | equōs. |
| main subject | head verb | &nbsp; | accusative subject of indirect statement | infinitive | direct object of indirect statement |

 Take care not to translate this as “We think him to protect horses.” (because that does not make any sense!).

-   Add the particle “that” after the verb “think”
-   Change the Latin accusative to a subject “he,” not “him”
-   Change the infinite verb to a finite one: protects, not “to protect”
-   “We think that he protects the horses.”

##### Reflexive pronouns

A reflexive pronoun exists only in the predicate and refers to (or ‘reflects’) the subject of the main clause of a sentence. Therefore, if the subject changes, so does the meaning of the reflexive. You know most of the reflexive pronouns already, because they are generally the same as the personal pronouns.

For example, *amicus me videt* (A friend sees me) and *ego amicum videō* (I see a friend). *me* and *ego* are both personal pronouns, but we translate them different based on their case.

But, in translating *ego mē videō*, we would not say “I see me,” but “I see myself.” This is simply how the reflexive works for both first and second person pronouns:

*tū vidēs tē* (you see yourself)

*nōs vidēmus nōs* (we see ourselves)

*dōna tibi das* (you give gifts to yourself)

The situation is slightly different for 3rd person though. It may seem confusing at first, but it actually allows Latin be clearer than English.

The third person reflexive pronoun is:

| Nominative | -- |
| --- | --- |
| Genitive | suī |
| Dative | sibi |
| Accusative | sē |
| Ablative | sē |

\*Notes:

-   There is no nominative form. The reflexive refers to the subject, so it cannot be the subject. In the case of a predicate nominative, the Romans simply did not have an expression like “she is not herself today.” (The closest would use a something called an intensive adjective: *Gaia nōn vidētur ipsa*, but that would not be a common pattern.)
-   The reflexive pronoun reflects the gender the subject, so it can be masculine, feminine, or neuter
-   The reflexive also reflects the number of the subject, so it can be singular or plural

For example:

*eum vident*: they see him

*eum videt*: he sees him (someone else)

*sē videt*: he sees himself

*sē vident*: they see themselves

The reflexive also commonly occurs in indirect statement when the subject of the main verb is the same as the subject of the infinitive. Take, for example the following English sentence: “She thinks that she is coming.” This is a vague sentence in English, because it’s unclear whether the she in “she thinks” and “she is coming” is the same person. Latin, however, has a reflexive in the third person, so that one can always know if the subject is the same as the predicate (reflexive pronoun) or different (personal pronoun).

| She | thinks | that | she | is coming |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **main subject** | **head verb** |  | **accusative subject of indirect statement** | **infinitive** |
| ea | putat | &nbsp; | sē (the subject) | venīre |
| ea | putat | &nbsp; | eam (not the subject) | venīre |

Because English is ambiguous, both sentences can mean “she thinks that she is coming,” and we simply don’t know which version to understand.

Latin, though, is clear.

-   *ea putat* ***sē*** *venīre* (she \*Lucretia\* thinks that she \*Lucretia\* is coming)
-   *ea putat* ***eām*** *venīre* (she \*Lucretia\* thinks that she \*Julia\* is coming).

##### Tense of Indirect Statements

The infinitive of the indirect statement is relative to the tense of the primary (head) verb. Therefore, whether the primary verb is present, imperfect, or perfect tense, a present infinitve will happen *at the same time*. Soon you will learn perfect and future infinitives, but for now practice translating different tenses with the present infinitive:

-   *ea putat* (present) *sē venīre*: she *thinks* that she *is coming*
-   *ea* *putābat* (imperfect) *sē venīre*: she *was thinking* that she *was coming*
-   *ea putāvit* (perfect) *sē venīre*: she *thought* that she was coming

No matter the tense of the primary action (she thinks, she was thinking, or she thought), the tense of the infinitive shows an action happening at that time. Even though *venire* stays present tense, its translated tense depends on the main verb.

# Dependent Clauses

### Principles of Grammar

Students of Latin often have difficulty with the number of clauses that are in a typical sentence. Whereas English speakers and writers tend to use many independent clauses with (and sometimes without) coordinating conjunctions, the Classical Latin authors make more use of dependent clauses and subordinating conjunctions. A dependent clause still has a subject (what the clause is about) and predicate (the verb and what the subject is/does), but it does not express a complete thought, so it cannot stand on its own.

There are many types of dependent clauses, but observe just a few:

-   I heard *what you said*. (noun clause)
    -   subject (you)
    -   predicate (said)
    -   functions to replace a noun—the noise that the speaker heard
-   I’ll see you *when you get here*. (adverb clause)
    -   subject (you)
    -   predicate (get here)
    -   replaces an adverb—when the action happens

Because the two languages often differ in this respect, it can be challenging to reconcile Latin with an English translation. In order to achieve this, you must identify sentence segments (prepositional phrases, independent clauses, and especially dependent clauses). If you treat each segment like an integral part of the sentence, it becomes easier to understand the sentence as a whole. As you are reading, note that there is a rule found in most Latin, with little exception: ***dependent clauses can interrupt independent clauses, but they cannot be interrupted by independent clauses***. Furthermore, dependent clauses can interrupt other dependent clauses, but each must finish before the previous clause resumes. The rule exists in English too, we just tend to take it for granted.

Take for example the following sentence:

Caesar, when the troops who conquered Gaul arrived, was delighted.

The independent clause, *Caesar was delighted*, is interrupted by a dependent clause, *when the troops arrived*. That dependent clause is interrupted by another dependent clause, *who conquered Gaul*. By paying close attention to this simple structure, it becomes much easier to read lengthy Latin sentences. Likewise, the sentence would mean something completely different if it said “Caesar conquered the delighted troops.” It is important to keep segment separate to keep the translations accurate.

There are several types of dependent clauses, and many belong in the subjunctive mood (to be learned later). The two we will learn about in this chapter are *temporal clauses* and *relative clauses*.

-   Temporal clauses describe a relationship in time with the independent clause: *When he arrives, we will celebrate*. Or *Before he arrives, we will celebrate*.
-   Relative clauses generally describe what person or thing the main clause is about: *The leader who arrived first*. Or *We found the men who escaped*.

### Principles of Latin

Temporal clauses begin with a subordinating conjunction or adverb (for our purposes, we’ll just identify all of these as subordinating conjunctions).

The most common are *cum* (when), *antequam* (before), *postquam* (after), and *dum* (while). *Cum* can also be a preposition that takes the ablative (accompaniment), but context can help determine which part of speech it is. The subordinating conjunction *ubi* can mean “when” or “where,” depending on context.

These clauses can be joined to the beginning or end of an independent clause, or they can be inserted in the middle. As always, the temporal clause must end before the independent clause resumes.

Relative clauses typically begin with a relative pronoun—sometimes with a preposition and relative if necessary—that refers to another word within the main clause. Relative pronouns are translated as *which, that*, *who, whose,* or *whom*, depending on their use the case. The difference between which/that and who/whose/whom depends on the **antecedent**. This is the word that the pronoun replaces or renames. Personal pronouns also have antecedents, but relative pronouns typically refer to an antecedent in the independent clause. The relative must have the same gender and number as the antecedent, but its case depends on its function in the relative clause.

Examples:

-   locī quōs vidēmus in montibus iacent
    -   The places that we see lie in the mountains
    -   *loci* (places): masculine, plural; nominative subject of independent clause
    -   *quos* (that): masculine, plural; accusative object of relative clause
-   Via in quā ambulāmus longa est
    -   The road on which we walk is long
    -   via (roads): feminine, singular; nominative subject of independent clause
    -   *in qua* (on which): feminine singular; ablative (location) in relative clause
-   verba cognoscunt quae canimus
    -   They recognize the words that we sing
    -   *verba* (words): accusative object of independent clause
    -   *quae* (that): accusative object of relative clause
-   Puerī puellaeque quī legere possunt ā hōminibus amantur
    -   Boys and girls who are able to read are loved by people
    -   *pueri puellaeque* (boys and girls): nominative subject of independent clause
    -   *qui* (who): nominative subject of relative clause

## Chapter 12 Vocabulary

You will begin to learn Latin proper names in this chapter. Romans typically had three names. The first name (praenomen) was just a common name. In fact, there were only about sixteen names used with any frequency, and they were typically abbreviated. You’ll learn the name and abbreviation for some of these. The second name (nomen) was a family name. Some names were held in great honor and being born or adopted into one of these names could advance one’s career (e.g., Brutus, Scipio). The third name (cognomen) was typically a name granted to someone as an honor or characteristic.

For Gaius Iulius Caesar, then. Gaius (abbreviated C. because in early Roman times it was Caius) was a common name, Iulius was his family name, and Caesar was his nickname. Supposedly he was born by Caesarian section (caedo, caedere, cecidi, caesus, to cut). This story seems unlikely, though, and another author claims that he was born with a head full of hair (*caesaries*). All sources are uncertain, though, and it is also possible that he received the nickname from someone else in his family.

Proper nouns in Latin decline just like common nouns. Most masculine names were second or third declension; feminine names were often first declension versions of the family name. Thus females in the family of Gaius Iulius Caesar would usually just have been known to historians as Julia (a fact that makes it difficult for modern historians to figure out which Julia did what).

Because we do not decline proper nouns in English, we always translate inflected names as the nominative:

Caesar vocat = Caesar calls

arma Caesaris = Caesar’s weapons

vocat Caesarem = he calls Caesar

# Adjectives and Participles

### Principles of Grammar

Participles are commonly used but not always understood. A participle, by definition, is a verbal adjective. Like a verb, it has some verbal elements (tense, voice). Since it is also an adjective, it has to agree with a noun in case, number, and gender (see also: **predicative participles**). Participles, like infinitive verbs have tense relative (prior, simultaneous, oe subsequent) to the main verb, not absolute time (perfect, present, future).

Compare two sentences:

*The* ***fast*** *horse ran toward the* ***tall*** *gate*

*The galloping horse ran toward the broken gate*

In the sentence *The fast horse ran toward the tall gate*, the adjectives *fast* and *tall* modify the nouns *horse* and *gate*, respectively. We know that the horse is fast and the gate tall because of word order.

If we replace the adjectives with participles, we would get something like: *The galloping horse ran toward the broken gate*. *Galloping* and *broken* both come from verbs (to gallop, to break), but now they describe nouns, and are therefore verbs that come from adjectives (verbal adjectives = participles).

Note too the verbal elements of participles. The first, *galloping*, is present, because it happens at the same time as *ran*. It is also active, because the horse is performing the action. *Broken*, on the other hand, implies that the gate has already been broken (complete action), so it is a perfect participle. Likewise, the gate received that action, so it is passive.

Note: English uses participles and helping verbs to create different tenses and voices, so pay attention to what a word is doing, not just what it looks like. We have seen this in the formation of imperfect (I was calling) and passive (I was called). The difference between *the galloping horse* and *the horse was galloping* is small, but significant. One is a clause, the other is not.

### Principles of Latin

Like English, Latin has a present active participle and a perfect passive participle. Latin also has future participles that will be introduced later.

Present Active participle: To form the present active participle, start with the present stem (since that gives you everything you need for the present system). If you add –ns to the nominative and –ntis to the genitive, you’ll have a third declension adjective (also introduced in this chapter) that forms the present participle. 4^th^ and 3^rd^ –io verbs add an *i* to the present stem.

Perfect passive participle: is simply the fourth principal part, but declined like magnus, magna, magnum. Latin does not have a present passive or perfect active participle, so there is no way to say “*being broken*” or “*having ran*.” \*see **deponent verbs** below\*

Adjectives: In addition to “verbal” adjectives, there are a few new types of adjectives to learn. Like all adjectives, they agree with a noun in case, number, and gender, but they do not look exactly like the bonus, bona, bonum type adjective we’ve seen so far.

–ius adjectives: These nine adjectives, most of them pertaining to quantity (one, another, neither), look like a typical 1/2 adjective, but the genitive and dative singular forms are the same as demonstratives like *ille*. They are otherwise like the other adjectives you have learned so far. They are most commonly remembered by the acrostic **UNUS NAUTA:**

-   Ūnus, ūna, ūnum: one
-   Nūllus, nūlla, nūllum: none
-   Uter, utra, utrum: either
-   Sōlus, sōla, sōlum: alone
-   Neuter, neutra, neutrum: neither
-   Alter, altera, alterum: one (of two) \* often appears twice (the one…., the other….)
-   Ūllus, ūlla, ūllum: any
-   Tōtus, tōta, tōtum: the whole, entire
-   Alius, alia, aliud: other

Like all adjectives, possessives have two parts: the stem gives the meaning (*mine, ours, yours*), while the ending agrees with a noun in case, number, and gender. This means that the gender of “mine” or “my” has nothing to do with the gender of the possessor, only what the adjective modifies. For example, in the *Vulgate*—the Latin translation of the Bible—Jesus says to his disciples during the last supper: *accipite, comoedite; hoc est corpus meum* (take and eat, this is my body). Both *hoc* and the possessive adjective *meum* are all neuter simply because the grammatical gender of *corpus* is neuter. As a fun side note, the notion of bread changing into the body of Christ led to the phrase “*hoc est corpus*” changed into “hokus pokus” when medieval magicians performed their acts.

Reflexive Possessive Adjectives: In addition to using the genitive of possession for 3rd person pronouns (*eius, eōrum, eārum*, and similar), the Romans had a second type of possessive related to the reflexive pronoun learned previously. This difference, which is often unclear in English, is helpful for communication.

In the sentence “Titus and Brutus walked in a field, and Titus saw his horse,” it can be unclear whether Titus saw his own horse or the horse of Brutus. This difficulty does not exist in Latin, which has two different words for the “his” used above.

**Suus, sua, suum** is the reflexive possessive adjective. It works just like the adjectives above, but it refers back to the subject as the possessor. Thus:

Titus Brutusque in agrō ambulāvērunt.

Titus suum equum videt = Titus sees his own horse.  
Titus eius equum videt = Titus sees his (someone else’s) horse.

Unlike English, most—but not all—participles modify whole clauses rather than individual nouns (see **attributive** and **predicative** participles in the index).

The sentence below can be translated several ways, depending on context:

Equus a multīs **petitus** ad viam currit. The horse, sought by many, runs toward the road.

The horse that was sought by many runs toward the road.

Because the horse was sought by many it ran toward the road.

The horse, when it was sought by many, ran toward the road.

In the first example, the perfect participle *petitus* is taken to modify the noun horse. In the other examples, although the participle agrees with *equus*, it is taken to modify the dependent clause.

Caesar, illōs **ducēns**, Romam relinquit. Caesar, leading those men, leaves Rome.

Caesar, who is leading those men, leaves Rome.

While Caesar is leading those men, he leaves Rome.

Although it’s difficult to choose the right sense of the participle when translating individual sentences, context will make it easier once you read them in passages.

Segmenting: Participles can mark sentence segments just like verbs, punctuation, conjunctions, and prepositions. Participles are often used to govern an independent clause, so they will contain the same place/time/means constructions as the verbs from which they come.

# Ablative Absolute and uses of the Dative

### Principles of Grammar

Participles are verbal adjectives and have therefore taken the traits of both. Some participles are more descriptive of nouns (e.g., “the broken pot”), and some have more a verbal force and govern clauses (e.g., “those caught breaking any pots in the house will be punished”).

One special use of the participle is an **ablative absolute. This** is a common dependent clause in Latin and is therefore a segment that can interrupt an independent clause. These clauses often replace other dependent clauses that would require a subordinating conjunction (like *cum*, *postquam*, etc.). Since the participle does all the work, it is up to you as the translator to decide what the relationship of the ablative absolute is to the rest of the sentence (does it mean ‘since’, ‘although’, ‘when’?). Another basic rule of the ablative absolute is that it does not share a subject or predicate with the main clause (it is *absolved/absolute*) from the rest of the clause. Therefore *With the city having been captured, the troops departed* is an ablative absolute, but *With the city having been captured, the troops entered it* could not be one (“city” is part of the ablative absolute and the main clause)

Another ablative use is the **ablative of manner**. This construction is often translated using the English preposition “with,” but unlike the other ablatives you’ve learned, it describes the way in which an action occurs. For example:

-   racers run *with friends* (**accompaniment**)
-   racers run *with their legs* (**means/instrument**)
-   racers run *with great speed* (**manner**)

In a sense, this ablative works like an adverb (“racers run with great speed” is about the same as “racers run very quickly”). The most common use of this ablative is (hopefully) heard at commencement if you graduate *magna cum laude* (“with great praise”).  

### Principles of Latin

The ablative absolute can be formed either with a present active or perfect passive participle. In this construction, the ablative singular is typically *–e* instead of *–ī*:

When you first encounter this construction, a simple formula can help you translate. First, identify the participle as present active or perfect passive.

-   Present active: “with \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_(noun) \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ing (participle)”
    -   puellā vocante (with the girl calling)
    -   puellīs vocantibus (with the girls calling)
-   Perfect passive: “with \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_(noun) having been \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed (participle)”
    -   puellā vocātā (with the girl having been called)
    -   puellīs vocātīs (with the girls having been called)

  
These translations often feel a bit awkward, but they help you establish the grammatical patterns and rules. Once you are more familiar with the formula, experiment with different ways to translate these constructions. The most important part of translation is to preserve the active or passive voice of the participle:

*puellā illum vocante*

-   because the girl is calling him
-   since the girl is calling him
-   while the girl is calling him
-   if the girl is calling him
-   **(**not**)** the girl is called by him
-   **(NOT) he called the girl**

An ablative absolute may also have objects, clauses, or phrases inside the clause. Typically the noun and participle will stand at the beginning and end of the clause, and it follows the same rules as other dependent clauses (it can interrupt an independent clause, but it can’t be interrupted by one):

-   puellā eum vocante (with the girl calling him)
-   puellā suam matrem vocante (with the girl calling her mother)

Most ablative absolutes start with nouns and end with participles, but that is a tendency, not a rule.

Dative: The most common uses of the dative, besides indirect object, are reference and special adjectives.

Dative of reference/interest There are a few related uses of the dative that show who is personally connected with a statement or fact.

-   It can indicate from whose perspective the statement is true *illī virō silva magna visa est* (“for that man, the forest seemed large”)
-   Especially with body parts, it can show a close connection, and often translated with the English possessive. *membra illī feminae* (“that woman’s limbs”)

Dative with special adjectives occurs with adjectives that would naturally take a to/for in English as well. Adjectives like easy, difficult, fitting, etc. often appear with a dative that explains to whom or in what way the adjective is easy, etc. Several of these are included in the chapter vocabulary.

# Degrees of Adjectives and the Future Tense

### Principles of English

Comparison: Adjectives come in three **degrees**. They are positive, comparative, and superlative. By “positive,” we do not mean “good,” just “baseline.” The positive degree is the form of adjective you’ve already learned: good, long, happy, sad. The comparative degree (naturally) compares an adjective to something else. This can appear in several forms in English: sadder; kind of sad; rather sad; etc. You do not always need to translate the comparative as *sadder* or *more sad*. The superlative degree is the strongest form of the adjective. It also can appear in several forms in English: very sad; terribly sad; saddest.^[\[4\]](#footnote-4)^

The future tense relates information that will happen at a later time. Typically the future tense is best translated as “will \_\_\_\_” (active) or “will be \_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed” (passive).

### Principles of Latin

Review 3^rd^ declension adjectives, especially two-ending adjectives like *omnis, omne* and *gravis, grave* (Chapter 13).

To form the comparative and superlative forms of adjectives, you must first find the stem. As usual, that always comes from the second word in the vocabulary. So for 1^st^/2^nd^ adjectives (e.g., *longus, longa, longum*: long/tall), the stem comes from the feminine singular (*long-*). For 3^rd^ adjectives (e.g., *tristis, triste*: sad), the stem comes from (in this case) the neuter (*trist-*). For other 3^rd^ declension adjectives (e.g., acer, acris, acre: sharp, fierce; potens (potentis): powerful), the stem still comes from the second form (*acr-* and *potent-*, respectively).

## Comparison

##### Comparative

To form the comparative, take the stem and create a 2 termination, 3^rd^ declension adjective. No matter what declension the positive degree (original adjective) is, its comparative will be third declension. The M/F ending will be –*ior*, and the neuter ending will be –*ius*.

for the adjective *longus, longa, longum*, the comparative is:

To form the superlative, take the stem and add *–issimus* (m), *\-issima* (f), *\-issimum* (n). No matter the declension of the positive degree, all superlatives are 1/2 declension adjectives.

##### ****Ablative of comparison****:

Sometimes in comparison, Latin puts the second noun or adjective in the ablative to show comparison:

*illa puella fortior est illō puerō.*

*illī puerī fortiorēs sunt illīs puellīs.*

##### ****Comparison with quam****:

More commonly, though, the coordinating conjunction *quam* is used, and the two words being compared stay in the same case:

*illa puella fortior est quam ille puer.*

*Caesar dicit se ambulāre celeriorem quam Lucium.*

## Future and Future Perfect Tense

In English, helping verbs “will” or “shall” are used to make a verb future tense. In Latin, the tense marker or a vowel change without a helping verb creates the future tense.

The rules vary slightly based on the conjugation. As is usual, the 1^st^ and 2^nd^ conjugation are very similar. For these, the tense indicator *–bi-* is used. Exceptions come from previously noted tendencies. The “i” is often contracted into the “o” in first person singular, and the Romans tended to avoid “int” as an ending.

\*magis … quam introduces an adverbial comparison. Instead of a noun being taller, faster, heavier, etc., this construction compares to actions (e.g., “that horse runs *faster than* the dog;” not “that horse is *faster…*” For this construction, *magis* typically appears net to the basic word, while *quam* appears next to the comparative word. Word order varies, but the simplest way to translate is to put the English words “more than” between the two verbs (so, *ea longius magis videt is quam currere potest* = “she sees further than he is able to run”).

# 4^th^ and 5^th^ Declension; Vocative and Locative case; Rest of the Perfect System

### Principles of Grammar

Even though the 4^th^ and 5^th^ declensions are less common, they do include some of the most important words (house, thing, day), and their endings should be memorized. The **Vocative** case, from *vocō*, *vocāre*, is used for direct address. The **Locative** case, from *locus, locī*, is used in special circumstances to show location. It is like the Ablative use of location, but only occurs with names of some places, especially cities (e.g., *Roma; Athenae*), and the nouns *domus*, *humus*, and *rus* (see vocabulary below). The formation for the locative varies, but it typically looks like the Genitive or Dative. The locative form will be given with each noun.

The final two tenses for you to learn are the pluperfect and future perfect. The **pluperfect** tense refers to an action that is already complete in the past and uses the English helping verb *had*. E.g., “He bought (perfect) the car after he *had saved* (pluperfect) all his money.” The **future perfect** expresses an action that will be complete in the future and uses the English helping verb *will have*. E.g., “He *will have saved* (future perfect) his money before he *will buy* (future) a car.”

### Principles of Latin

Words that take the Locative have another rule. They still use accusative to show motion toward and ablative to show motion away, but they do not use prepositions like *ad, ex,* or *de*. Thus the Romans would write *venimus ad urbem* (we are coming to the city), but *venimus Romam* (we are coming to Rome). This is similar in English with the noun domus (home). We say “we are coming home,” not “we are coming to home.”

The genitive singular ending—remember that this determines the declension of a word—for 4^th^ declension is –*ūs*. For 5^th^ declension the ending is –*ēī* or –*eī*. The difference between the two depends on whether the letter before the ending is a vowel or consonant, but you just need to remember that either is possible.

As with all verbs, the future perfect and pluperfect are based on the third principal part (active) and fourth principal part (passive) of the verb.

Cases and Declensions

Formation of the Locative: Remember that this is only for a limited group of rules (most importantly, names of places, and the nouns *domus, humus,* and *rus*). The formation of the locative is a little complex, but the pattern of the locative is straightforward. If you see a word that falls into a locative category and it has an ending that looks like the genitive or ablative of that case, it is most likely a locative. A complete list of locative endings is in the index.

Formation of the vocative: For most words, the vocative is the same as the nominative. There are two exceptions, both of which only occur in the 2^nd^ declension. If a noun ends in *–us* in the nominative, its vocative is –*e*. If a noun ends in –*ius*, its vocative is -*ī*. This occurs most often for proper names (Titus becomes *Tite* in the vocative).

Common examples:

-   For puella, puellae (f), the vocative forms are puella (singular) and puellae (plural)
-   For amicus, amicī (m), the vocative forms are amic**e** (singular) and amicī (plural)
-   For filius, filiī (m), the vocative forms are fil**ī** (singular) and filiī (plural)

Perfect System Forms

Perfect Infinitives: In addition to the present active and present passive infinitives, Latin has perfect infinitives and future infinitives (chapter 17, below).

As with all Latin verbs, anything in the perfect active system comes from the 3^rd^ principal part. Although it technically means “to have \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed,” it is more often used in indirect statement (see exercises below). The perfect passive infinitive, like everything in the perfect passive system, comes from the fourth principal part. It too will most often occur in indirect statement, but can be translated individually as “to have been \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed.”

To form the perfect active infinitive, take the 3^rd^ principal part of the verb (*vocāvī, vīdī, rēxī, cēpī, audīvī*), and remove the final *–ī*. Then, add the ending *–isse*. This works for all regular verbs.

1^st^ conjugation: *vocāv* + *isse* vocāvisse (to have called)

2^nd^ conjugation: *vīd* + *isse* vīdisse (to have seen)

3^rd^ conjugation: *rēx* + *isse* rēxisse (to have ruled)

3^rd^ –io conjugation: *cēp* + *isse* cēpisse (to have seized)

4^th^ conjugation: *audīv* + *isse* audīvisse (to have heard)

To form the perfect passive infinitive, simply add the infinitive of *sum* to the 4^th^ principal part. This should be intuitive, since you add an inflected form of *sum* to make the perfect passive indicative.

**\*Note:** like all perfect passive verbs, the gender and number of the infinitive can change to reflect the subject. Because you will most often see the perfect passive infinitive in indirect statement, you will often see it with accusative endings.

1^st^ conjugation: vocātus, -a, -um esse (to have been called)

2^nd^ conjugation: vīsus, -a, -um esse (to have been seen; to have seemed)

3^rd^ conjugation: rectus, -a, -um esse (to have been ruled)

3^rd^ –io conjugation: captus, -a, -um esse (to have been seized)

4^th^ conjugation: auditus, -a, -um esse (to have been heard)

# Gerunds; Future Participles and Infinitives

### Principles of Grammar

The next two chapters introduce several less typical patterns and grammatical functions of verbs.

Aside from the future tense of finite verbs, there are also future tense participles and infinitives. Like their present and perfect counterparts, the future infinitives typically show time relative to the main verb. Just like perfect infinitives show action *prior* *to* the verb and present infinitives show action *the same time as* the main verbs, future forms show action *subsequent to* the main verb. The practicalities on how to translate future forms are in the examples below.

You’ve already seen that verbs can be used to form adjectives (participles). In this chapter you’ll also learn about nouns that can be formed from verbs: **gerunds**.

##### Future Participles

The future active participle is not terribly common in Classical Latin. It can serve as a replacement for future tenses or to be more emphatic (*vir nōn tentūrus urbem* “a man *not about to hold* the city”), and it also appears in later poetry.

The future passive participle (also called the **gerundive**) is more common. It is also noteworty in that it has additional uses beyond the other participles you have learned. The gerundive, which seems like it should be translated “will be \_\_\_\_\_ed,” more often connotes need or obligation (“should be \_\_\_\_\_ed”). This can be through a variety of uses but is often seen in a special construction called the **passive periphrastic**.

##### Infinitives

###### Infinitive as a noun

A use of the infinitive that does not require another, finite, verb (like complementary or indirect statement) is the **infinitive as a noun**. In this construction, the infinitive can only serve as a singular subject. It can be translated either (e.g.,) *to call* or *calling*. When the infinitive is used as a noun, is form is always neuter singular.

-   *To see* the countryside is my dream.
-   Seeing the countryside is relaxing.

Note that the future passive infinitive is rare and does not need to be memorized at this point. The future active infinitive is used almost exclusively with indirect statement. Since the translation of the infinitive is relative to the tense of the main verb in this construction, its translation will vary:

-   Present tense main verb: Caesar dicit eam vocātūram esse = Caesar *says* that she *will* call.
-   Imperfect/perfect tense main verb: Caesar dīxit eam vocātūram esse = Caesar *said* that she *would* call.

##### Gerunds

The gerundive (gerund*ive* = adject*ive*) is related to another type of word called the gerund. The gerundive (a participle) is a verbal adjective, while the gerund is a verbal noun. The noun is usually expressed in English with the suffix -ing. For example, I learn best *by reading*. (Note well: this is different from the participial phrase The person *reading* this book will learn.) The gerund is related to the new form of the infinitive introduced in this chapter, the infinitive as a noun. The infinitive as a noun can only be the subject of a sentence, so the gerund supplies the other cases:

-   Nominative: *To win (winning)* feels great (infinitive as a noun)
-   Genitive: A desire *of winning* makes me work hard
-   Dative: This type of practice is good for *winning*
-   Accusative: Let’s go there *to win* (accusative gerunds only exist with the prepositions *ad* or *in*)
-   Ablative: We earned praise from everyone *by winning* the tournament

### Principles of Latin

##### Participle Formation:

You have already learned the present and perfect participles. The future active participle is very similar to the future active infinitive:

| Participles | Active | Passive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Present | vocāns, vocāntis   vidēns, videntis   agēns, agentis   capiēns, capientis   audiēns, audientis | --- |
| Perfect | --- | vocātus, -a, -um |
| Future | vocātūrus, -a, -um | vocāndus, -a, -um   videndus, -a, -um   agendus, -a, -um   capiendus, -a, -um   audiendus, -a, -um |

The future passive participle also give us the form of gerunds and gerundives (both words are in fact formed from the future passive participle of gerere).

The gerundive is formed from the present stem. The ending *\-undus, -unda, -undum* is added to form a 1^st^/2^nd^ declension adjectives (just like bonus, bona, bonum). Translations of the gerundive vary (see below), but they often have the sense of necessity. Consider common derivatives of gerundives: agenda (from *agere*)—items that should be done; referendum (from *referre*)—a vote taken back to the entire electorate; legend (from *legere*)—a book/movie that should be read; Amanda (from *amare*)—someone loveable.

##### Infinitive Formation

You have already learned the present and perfect infinitives and participles. In this section, you’ll learn how to form the future as well.

Remember that only in the present do infinitives differ based on conjugation. Perfect and future infinitives are the same for all conjugation (1^st^ is given here as reference).

| Infinitives | Active | Passive |
| --- | --- | --- |

To form the future infinitive, simply add the helping verb to the future participle. \*The future passive infinitive, which is formed by taking the 4^th^ principal part ending in –um (a form also called the **supine**) and and adding the helping verb *īrī*, is uncommon and does not need to be memorized.\*

The future infinitive of *sum* is *futurum esse*. This is often shortened into *fore*, which can stand for any form of the future infinitive (*futurum esse*, *futuram esse*, *futurōs esse*, etc.).

Use of Gerunds and Gerundives

One of the most common (and somewhat specialized) use is a pattern used for gerunds and gerundives (and although not covered here, in supines).

Pattern recognition—a verb of motion (*venīre, currere,* etc.), plus the preposition *ad*, plus an accusative gerund or gerundive shows purpose: *cucurrimus ad templum servāndum*: “we ran to protect the temple”

##### Gerundive as a replacement for the gerund

Since gerunds are verbal nouns, they can take direct objects (discimus legendō librōs = we learn by reading books; advēnimus ad capiendum urbes = we have come to capture the cities). This was form found in early Latin and in Late/Medieval Latin. Classical Latin, though, authors often replaced the gerund with a gerundive, then changed the direct object to match the case that the gerund would have had (note that this is an impossible construction in English). This seems complicated, but identifying and translating it is fairly intuitive after some practice.

Instead of *discimus legendō librōs*, Classical authors would write *discimus legendīs librīs*. Since nouns and adjectives must agree in case, number, and gender, the case is determined by gerund form (which is now a gerundive), while the gender and number are determined by the noun. The translation is literally “we learn by book readings,” but we translate it the same as we would a gerund: “we learn by reading books.”

##### Passive periphrastic

Sometimes the gerundive is combined with form of *sum*. This combination makes an even stronger sense of obligation. In this formation, the Ablative of Agent is replaced with a Dative of Agent (without a preposition).

spes habenda est = hope *must be* held.

deī colendī sunt = the gods *must be* worshipped.

littera scribenda est mihi = the letter *must be* written by me. (or) I must write the letter.

### Principles of Latin

Listed below are the different types of funky verbs. Although the list can be daunting at first, remember that there is no new grammar to learn; these verbs just have their own special pattern. The vocabulary entry will also clue you in to what pattern you should expect.

Intransitive verbs that take dative objects: These are simply verbs that do not have typical transitive properties. A good example in Latin and English are the verbs *obey* and *study*. In English, we use the verb transitively “I obey my professor and study my homework,” but we are not actually acting on the direct objects, in the same way we might say “I chew my food and pet my dog.” The professor and homework do not actually receive any action from the verb. In Latin, many verbs like this take Dative objects.

Some of these verbs (full vocabulary with principal parts is at the end of the chapter) are:

parēre: to obey (to be obedient *to* someone)  
placēre: to please (to be pleasing *to* someone)  
obstare: to hinder (to be a hindrance to someone)

nocēre: to harm (to be harmful to someone)

credere: to trust (to entrust yourself to someone)

Deponent verbs: These verbs are common in Latin (and other older languages). They look passive, but we translate them as active. These verbs still belong to the usual conjugations; their principal parts will just look passive. A first conjugation verb will appear in the dictionary like this: *hortor, hortārī, hortātus sum: to urge*. Since there is no passive form to these verbs, they do not have a typical fourth principal part. Like any other verbs, they can be transitive, intransitive, and even take indirect statements. The conjugation is determined by the infinitive, as usual, but the passive infinitive form: -ārī (1); -ērī (2); -ī (3^rd^, 3^rd^ –io); -īrī (4th)

Some of these verbs (a list of full vocabulary with principal parts is at the end of the chapter) are:

sequī: to follow

patī: to endure

precārī: to pray

Special Deponent Verbs: there are five verbs that obey the typical rules of deponent verbs (they look passive, but they are active), but they take *ablative objects instead of accusative direct objects*. The five special deponent verbs (full list of principal parts is below) are *potior* (to obtain, capture), *utor* (to use, enjoy), *fruor* (to enjoy, profit in), *fungor* (to perform, execute), and *vescor* (to eat). These five verbs, which all generally refer to gaining or using something, go by the acronym PUFF-V.

Semi Deponent Verbs: the last of the funky verbs are semi-deponent verbs. These are perhaps the funkiest of them all. These verbs are deponent only in the perfect system. For example, *audeō, audēre, ausus sum* (to dare), seems like a usual verb in the present, imperfect, and future, but in the perfect tenses it is deponent (so *ausus sum* means “I dared,” not “I have been dared”). Fortunately, there are not many of these verbs in Latin.

# Intro to Subjunctive, Independent clauses

### Principles of Grammar

Many students of Latin are unnecessarily frightened by the thought of learning the subjunctive. There are some new endings to learn, but they are easier to remember (and far more regular) than the indicative endings you have already learned. One difficulty is that subjunctives typically come in dependent clauses, which are less common in English, but they are something you have already started to master. Another difficulty is just that subjunctive in English is less common. If you think more about the patterns than all the intricacies of the subjunctive, you will be able to understand it quickly.

Subjunctive is a **mood** like indicative or imperative. The main difference is that while indicative *indicates* what is true and imperative commands someone to do something, the subjunctive deals with possibilities.

Instead of describing what the subject *is, is doing, or was doing*, the subjunctive describes what the subject *may be, wants to do, or would have done in other circumstances*. Another way to describe it is that the relationship between the subject and verb is *objective* in the indicative (based on reality) and *subjective* in the subjunctive (based on possibilities).

This does not describe every subjunctive, but patterns can determine when you’re likely to see a subjunctive and (more importantly) how to translate it.

Independent Subjunctive

There are four major independent uses of the subjunctive. We’ll look at these first, even though dependent uses are more common (since that’s a primary job of the subjunctive).

These are:

1.  Hortatory and/or jussive subjunctive: polite commands. Technically hortatory describes verbs in the first person and jussive describes 2^nd^ and 3^rd^ person verbs.
    1.  We should go there tomorrow.
    2.  Let me find the treasure.
    3.  Let them read the books.
2.  Optative subjunctive: a wish or prayer. These show what the subjects hopes will happen. Note that these are always from the point of view of the wisher, but will have the person and number of the actual subjects. *I hope they will find us* is therefore third person plural (“they will find”) not first person.
    1.  I hope that we go there tomorrow.
    2.  I wish to find the treasure.
    3.  If only they’ll read the books.
3.  Deliberative subjunctive: an internal question or a statement of internal doubt. This is closely related to the hortatory/jussive, but it is directed inward by the speaker.
    1.  Should we go there tomorrow?
    2.  Shall I find the treasure?
    3.  Should they read the books?
4.  Potential: possible action. Also closely related to the other independent subjunctives, the potential describes what the subject may do or is likely to do.
    1.  We may go there tomorrow.
    2.  Perhaps we’ll find the treasure.
    3.  They should read the books.

\*The examples above are all translations of hypothetical present subjunctives. Translations of other tenses will be addressed in the next two chapters. Note too that the translations of the subjunctive tend to refer to the future. All subjunctive tenses tend to “peek” into a tense (so the imperfect subjunctive addresses the present, etc.). There is therefore no such thing as a future subjunctive.

### Principles of Latin

Whether the subjunctive is independent or dependent, the formation is the same. Because of the subjective relationship between the subject and verb, there is not always a straightforward translation. For this chapter, we’ll mainly use the potential subjunctive, but remember that there are other ways to translate the verb based on pattern and context.

##### Independent Subjunctive patterns

Without context, it is difficult to know how to translate independent subjunctives. At times, the translation is ambiguous and up for interpretation. There are some additional vocabulary words that tell you what pattern to expect. These words do not have to be translated; they simply show the pattern. These words and their patterns should be memorized as part of your vocabulary.

-   *utinam*: indicates the next subjunctive verb is **optative**
-   *forsitan*: indicates that the next subjunctive verb is **potential**
-   A question mark at the end of a sentence (when the primary verb is subjunctive) shows that an editor has decided it is **deliberative**
-   None of these words are required for optative, potential, or deliberative subjunctives; they are like the signs that tell you to look out for deer, a railroad crossing, or falling rocks. Signs aren’t required for a deer to cross, but they alert you to their presence.

# Adverb Clauses in the Subjunctive

### Principles of Grammar

The subjunctive is often used for adverb clauses and noun clauses. In this chapter, you will learn the most common adverb clauses. An **adverb clause** is, as strange as it may sound, a clause that functions as an adverb: it modifies a verb in the main clause or another dependent clause. Here is an example:

The storm came quickly.

-   In this sentence, *quickly* is an adverb that describes the verb *came*. But the sentence can be more descriptive:

The storm came so quickly that we hid under a rock.

-   In this sentence, *so quickly that we hid under a rock* is still modifying the verb *came*, but it is also a dependent clause.

Adverbs clauses describe the purpose, result, or circumstance of a verb. When this happens, Latin typically uses the subjunctive, but when we translate to English the verb is often the same as the indicative. The pattern will indicate the correct translation.

There are four main adverbial clauses: purpose, result, *cum* clauses, and conditions. Each one uses the subjunctive, and (for the most part) they have specific markers to show you which pattern to use.

-   Purpose clauses answer the question: “why did the subject perform the action?” (e.g., “I study *so that I may pass the test*”)
-   Result clauses explain the outcome of an action (e.g., “I studied so hard *that I passed the test*”)
-   Cum clauses (e.g., “*Because I studied hard,* I passed the test”)
-   conditions (e.g., “*If I had studied hard, I would have passed the test*”)

### Principles of Latin

Adverb clauses use a subordinating conjunction (you learned about these with *cum* and relative clauses in chapter 12). For these clauses, though, the verb is in the subjunctive. Finding the pattern will help you translate the clause quickly and accurately.

-   Purpose clauses are typically marked with the conjunction *ut* or *ne* (*ne* takes the place of *non* in some subjunctive clauses).
    -   consul scribit *ut mutent* (the consul writes *so that they may change*)
    -   consul scribit *ne mutent* (the consul writes *so that they may not change*)
-   Result clauses are typically marked with the conjunction *ut* or *ut non*. This clause will usually (but not always) have a signal word (*ita*\-so, in such a way; *tantus*\-so much) in the main clause that will signal a result clause. A comprehensive list of these words is listed in the chapter vocabulary.
    -   consul **ita** scribit *ut mutent* (the consul writes in such a way *that they change*)
    -   consul **ita** scribit *ut non mutent* (the consul writes in such a way *that they do not change*)
-   Cum clauses start with the subordinating conjunction *cum* and show a relationship between the main clause and the dependent clause. There are three types of relationships, outlined below
-   Conditionals show an “if...then...” relationship between the main clause and the dependent clause. They include the subordinating conjunction *si* (if), but can be translated in a variety of ways outlined below.

##### Purpose Clause

These are the simplest and most common adverb clauses. They are marked by *ut* or *ne*.

\*Patterns: Note that if the verb in the main clause is present, future, or (sometimes perfect—translate “has \_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed” or “have \_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed”) the subjunctive will be present or perfect. If the verb is any other tense (and sometimes perfect—translate “\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed”), the subjunctive verb will be imperfect or pluperfect.

##### Result clause

These clauses will almost always have an adverb (*ita, sic, tam, adeo, talis, tantus, tot, totiens*) in the main clause to signal a consequence. They can be translated with the old, but mean middle school “your mom” joke (“your mom is *so* ugly, that she threw a boomerang and it refused to come back”). The adverb does not have to be there, but it will typically be used in this text so you can identify the pattern.

##### Cum clause

You have already learned one *cum* clause that uses an indicative verb. It’s called a temporal clause (*cum viam vident ambulabunt* “when they see the road they will walk”). There are three types of *cum* clauses that use subjunctive verbs. Context is the best way to decide how to translate, but sometimes there is room for interpretation.

-   Circumstantial clauses: these are the most common and most basic (*cum viam videant ambulant* “when they see the road, they walk”).
-   Causal clauses: context and your own interpretation will help, but these show a causal relationship between the two clauses (*cum viam videant ambulant* “because they see the road, they walk”).
-   Adversative clauses: occur when the independent clause shows something that happened despite the dependent clause (*cum viam videant ambulant* “even though they see the road, they walk”). These clauses often use the particle *tamen* in the dependent clause (*cum viam videant tamen ambulant*) to make sure the reader understands the pattern.

##### Conditions

These clauses require the most attention to patterns. Each clause has a subjunctive or indicative verb, and the tense and voice of the verb show you how to translate. We’ll start with 6 basic patterns. Each pattern has two clauses. The dependent clause (also called the **protasis**) is the “if” part of the condition and is started with *si* “if” or *nisi* “unless”. The independent clause (**apodosis) is the “then” part of the condition, and does not have any clause markers.**

| **indicative (real)** | protasis | apodosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| present general | present indicative | present indicative |
| past general | perfect indicative | perfect indicative |
| future more-vivid | future indicative | future indicative |
| **subjunctive (unreal)** |  |  |
| future less-vivid (should/would) | present subjunctive | present subjunctive |
| present contrafactual | imperfect subjunctive | imperfect subjunctive |
| past contrafactual | pluperfect subjunctive | pluperfect subjunctive |

Real:

-   present general: *si senātus regit, fallīmur*: If the senate rules, we are deceived.
-   past general: *si senātus rēxit, falsī sumus*: If the senate ruled, we were deceived.
-   future more-vivid: *si senātus reget, fallēmur*. If the senate (will) rule, we will be deceived.

Unreal:

-   future less-vivid (should/would): *si id aedificias, ei veniant*: Should you build it, they would come.
-   present contrafactual: *si id aedificeres, ei venirent*: Were you building it, they would come.
-   past contrafactual: *si id aedifecisses, ei vēnissent*: Had you built it, they would have come.

Many conditions you see in Latin are a combination (a perfect subjunctive in the protasis and a present indicative in the apodosis. Just remember the translation rule for each part of the condition and combine.

# Noun clauses

### Principles of Grammar

Adverb clauses replace adverbs, but there are also clauses (predictably called **Noun Clauses**) that replace nouns. You saw this in chapter 12 when we discussed dependent clauses (I heard *what you said.*) These clauses are some of the most difficult clauses in Latin, unless you look at them by their patterns.

The most common noun clauses are:

-   Indirect question—these sometimes report a question “he asks why you went there,” but more often answer a question “he knows why you went there,” or “he wonders why you went there.”
    -   these have a head verb, usually one of asking, requesting, or wondering
    -   a question word (who?, why? where?)
    -   a subjunctive verb (remember that if the main verb is present or future, the subjunctive verb will be present or perfect; if the main verb is imperfect or pluperfect, the subjunctive will be imperfect or pluperfect)
-   Indirect command—these give a report of one person telling someone to do something (e.g., “we urge you to find it,” or “we told you to find it.” Although this verb often has a head verb, it does not follow the same pattern as an indirect statement.
    -   a verb of speaking or commanding
    -   a subordinating conjunction (ut or ne)
    -   a subjunctive verb
-   Noun result clauses—like adverbial result clauses in the previous chapter, noun result clauses have a word or phrase in the main clause. Grammatically, often they can be translated the same as the indicative. An indicative clause “You found my phone” becomes a subjunctive clause when it’s the result of the main clause “It just so happens that you found my phone” or “It’s common that you find my phone.”
    -   a “signal word” in the main clause. These will be in the dictionary
    -   a subordinating conjunction (ut, ut nōn, or ne)
    -   a subjunctive verb
-   Fear clauses—these are not common, but they are worth noting because they deviate from the normal pattern.
    -   a verb of fear (timēre, metuēre, verērī)
    -   a subordinating conjunction (ut or nē)
    -   a subjunctive verb
    -   when you translate these, just switch the meaning of *ut* and *ne* (timeō ut me videas = I am afraid that you do **not** see me)

### Principles of Latin

-   Indirect question—you have learned several vocabulary words related to indirect question, and they will be reintroduced in this chapter’s vocabulary. Most “q” words in Latin correspond to “who” words in English (e.g., *quo* = what and *quid* \= what)
-   Indirect command—the best way to translate these is with an English infinitive (frustrating because with indirect statement you have a Latin infinitive but you don’t translate it that way) Remember to follow the pattern, because this clause does usually look like a command (e.g., *dicit nōs equōs* ***sequī*** \= he says that we follow the horses; *dicit ut nōs equōs* ***sequāmus*** = he tells us to follow the horses \[that we follow the horses\])
-   Noun result clause—the pattern for these is the most difficult to catch because the main clause varies. If a subjunctive verb does not match any other pattern, it is probably a noun result: *reliquum est ut abeāmus* = all that remains is that we leave; *facitur ut eum videant* = it just so happens that they see him
-   Fear clause—a strange but simple clause. If the main verb is a verb of fear (see vocabulary), then *ut* means “that not” and *ne* means “that” *metuīmus* ***ne*** *hostēs advenīrent* = we were afraid **that** the enemies were arriving

Phrases that commonly start noun result clauses and should be added to your vocabulary: accidit + ut + subjunctive (it happens that \_\_\_\_); faciam + ut (I will make sure that \_\_\_); ita efficitur ut (it just so happened that \_\_\_); mos mihi est + ut (it is common for me to \_\_\_; contigit + ut (it turns out that \_\_\_); necesse est + ut (it is necessary that \_\_\_); reliquum est + ut (all that remains is that \_\_\_); fit ut (it just so happens that \_\_\_)

Question words that start indirect questions:

-   cur: why?
-   quis, quid: who, what? (declines like qui, quae, quod except in the neuter)
-   qualis, quale: of what kind?
-   quam: how?
-   quantum: how much?
-   quemadmodem: how the heck?
-   quotiens: how many times? how often?
-   ubi: where? when?
