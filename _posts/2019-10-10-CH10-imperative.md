### Principles of Grammar

Thus far we have covered 3 of the 5 attributes of verbs: person (1st, 2nd, 3rd); number (singular, plural), and tense (present, imperfect, and perfect). In this chapter, you’ll learn about the remaining two attributes.

Mood:

The indicative, in which the speaker is just “indicating” what he/she perceives, has been the mood of all verbs thus far. The imperative mood expresses the speaker’s will for something to happen. The most common imperative form (and the only one covered in this book) is the second person imperative. Although we don’t typically say “you” in English, it is implied in a phrase like “(you) Get the book for me.” Likewise, uses of the imperative are typically present tense and **active voice**.

Voice: In grammatical terms, voice refers to the relationship between the subject and the action. In Latin and English, the **passive voice** is used when the subject receives the action. Until now, we have only used sentences in which the subject performs the action (active) or describes the predicate (linking). Transitive verbs can be active or passive; intransitive verbs are rarely passive (it is impossible to “be remained”).

Passive voice can be frustrating and difficult for students in the beginning, but it is commonly used in Latin. In fact, learning the rules in Latin often helps students with their English use. Active and passive voice clauses can contain the same information, but they emphasize different parts.

For example:

-   Active: “The students see that book.” (the subject—students—performs the action)
-   Passive: “That book is seen by the students.” (the subject—book—receives the action)

In both sentences, the information is the same, but one focuses on the students, while the other stresses the book.

English forms passive voice with the past participle of the verb and a helping verb (in the example above, *seen* is the past participle and *is* is the helping verb). The helping verb changes the tense and number of the main verb, while the participle remains the same. **Remember that the presence of a participle or helping verb does not by itself indicate passive voice**.

The door is opened by the student. (present passive)

The door was opened by the student. (past passive)

The student was opening the door. (past active)

### Principles of Latin

The present imperative is formed from the present stem. Although other forms of the imperative exist, they are not common.

To create the singular imperative, you simply use the present stem (the infinitive without the –re). To make the imperative plural, you simply need to add –te. The 3rd and 3rd -io forms are (like always) slightly different:

Singular Plural

1st conjugation vocā (you) call! vocāte (y’all) all!

2nd conjugation vidē (you) see! vidēte (y’all) see!

3rd conjugation rege (you) act! **regite** (y’all) act!

3rd -io conjugation cape (you) seize! **capite** (y’all) seize!

4th conjugation audī (you) hear! audīte (y’all) hear!

Passive voice is also simple to form in Latin. For the present and imperfect tense, you’ll continue to use the present stem (just like for active voice). The 3rd principal part forms only the perfect active stem, so now you will use the 4th principal part to form the perfect passive tense.

For the present and imperfect, all of the usual rules apply to forming the stem and the tense. Latin just has different personal endings. So instead of

\-ō/-m -mus use the passive endings: -r -mur

\-s -tis -ris -mini

\-t -nt -tur -ntur

Present tense (add passive endings to the present stem)

In English: I am called, am being called; I am seen, I am being seen, etc.

| 1st conjugation | 2nd conjugation | 3rd conjugation | 3rd -io conjugation | 4th conjugation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **vocor** | videor | regor | capior | audior |
| vocāris | vidēris | **regeris** | **caperis** | audīris |
| vocatur | vidētur | regitur | capitur | auditur |
| vocāmur | vidēmur | regimur | capimur | audīmur |
| vocāminī | vidēminī | regiminī | capiminī | audīminī |
| vocantur | videntur | reguntur | capiuntur | audiuntur |

Notes:

\*For the first conjugation, like in the active voice, the first person singular is contracted, so the form is **vocor**, not vocaor.

\*As always, the 3rd and 3rd -io forms have some irregularities.

\*The Romans did not like the sound of -ir- in the middle of words (just like they didn’t like the sound of -int at the end of words), so those forms both become -eris.

| 1st conjugation | 2nd conjugation | 3rd conjugation | 3rd -io conjugation | 4th conjugation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| vocābar | vidēbar | regēbar | capiēbar | audiēbar |
| vocābāris | vidēbāris | regēbāris | capiēbāris | audiēbāris |
| vocābatur | vidēbatur | regēbatur | capiēbatur | audiēbatur |
| vocābāmur | vidēbāmur | regēbāmur | capiēbāmur | audiēbāmur |

\*As with the active forms, the “i” in 3rd and 3rd -io verbs turns into an “e.” 3rd -io verbs also have the stem change into “ie” instead of just “e.”

Perfect tense (add ‘be’ verbs to the fourth principal part)

In English: I was called, I have been called

| 1st conjugation | 2nd conjugation | 3rd conjugation | 3rd -io conjugation | 4th conjugation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| vocātus, a, um sum | vīsus, a, um sum | rectus, a, um sum | captus, a, um sum | auditus, a, um sum |
| vocātus, -a, -um es | vīsus, -a, -um es | rectus, a, um es | captus, a, um es | auditus, a, um es |
| vocātus, -a, -um est | vīsus, -a, -um est | rectus, a, um est | captus, a, um est | auditus, a, um est |
| vocātī, -ae, a sumus | vīsī, -ae, -a sumus | rectī, ae, a sumus | captī, ae, a sumus | auditī, ae, a sumus |
| vocātī, -ae, a estis | vīsī, -ae, -a estis | rectī, ae, a estis | captī, ae, a estis | auditī, ae, a estis |
| vocātī, -ae, a sunt | vīsī, -ae, -a sunt | rectī, ae, a sunt | captī, ae, a sunt | auditī, ae, a sunt |

Notes:

\*Verbs in the perfect passive system are the only ones that need to agree with their subject in gender, not just person and number. They will always look like bonus, bona, bonum type adjectives (more on this when you learn about participles).

##### Ablative of agent

This ablative use occurs with a passive voice verb to show the person or animal that performs the action. In an active voice pattern, the subject performs the action, so this case use is unique to the passive voice pattern.

Agent is like Ablative of Means, but an Agent must be a person (or personified animal). And while Ablative of Means uses no preposition, Agent uses ā/ab.

Agent: *is vincitur* ***ā Caesare***: he is conquered ***by Caesar***

Means/instrument: *is vincitur* ***armīs***: he is conquered ***with weapons***

Pattern Recognition

In English, passive verbs always have a “be” helping verb (“it is thought,” “the ball was thrown,” “we have been seen”), but that “be” verb doesn’t make something passive (“he is thinking,” “the ball was rolling,” “we have been seeing”). Always focus on what the word is doing, not just what it might look like.

In Latin, this isn’t always the case. The pattern is \*subject\* (receives) \*verb\* (optional: by person (agent) or thing (means)
