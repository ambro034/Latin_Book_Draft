### Principles of Grammar

Participles are verbal adjectives and have therefore taken the traits of both. Some participles are more descriptive of nouns (e.g., “the broken pot”), and some have more a verbal force and govern clauses (e.g., “those caught breaking any pots in the house will be punished”).

One special use of the participle is an **ablative absolute. This** is a common dependent clause in Latin and is therefore a segment that can interrupt an independent clause. These clauses often replace other dependent clauses that would require a subordinating conjunction (like *cum*, *postquam*, etc.). Since the participle does all the work, it is up to you as the translator to decide what the relationship of the ablative absolute is to the rest of the sentence (does it mean ‘since’, ‘although’, ‘when’?). Another basic rule of the ablative absolute is that it does not share a subject or predicate with the main clause (it is *absolved/absolute*) from the rest of the clause. Therefore *With the city having been captured, the troops departed* is an ablative absolute, but *With the city having been captured, the troops entered it* could not be one (“city” is part of the ablative absolute and the main clause)

Another ablative use is the **ablative of manner**. This construction is often translated using the English preposition “with,” but unlike the other ablatives you’ve learned, it describes the way in which an action occurs. For example:

-   racers run *with friends* (**accompaniment**)
-   racers run *with their legs* (**means/instrument**)
-   racers run *with great speed* (**manner**)

In a sense, this ablative works like an adverb (“racers run with great speed” is about the same as “racers run very quickly”). The most common use of this ablative is (hopefully) heard at commencement if you graduate *magna cum laude* (“with great praise”).  

### Principles of Latin

The ablative absolute can be formed either with a present active or perfect passive participle. In this construction, the ablative singular is typically *–e* instead of *–ī*:

When you first encounter this construction, a simple formula can help you translate. First, identify the participle as present active or perfect passive.

-   Present active: “with \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_(noun) \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ing (participle)”
    -   puellā vocante (with the girl calling)
    -   puellīs vocantibus (with the girls calling)
-   Perfect passive: “with \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_(noun) having been \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed (participle)”
    -   puellā vocātā (with the girl having been called)
    -   puellīs vocātīs (with the girls having been called)

  
These translations often feel a bit awkward, but they help you establish the grammatical patterns and rules. Once you are more familiar with the formula, experiment with different ways to translate these constructions. The most important part of translation is to preserve the active or passive voice of the participle:

*puellā illum vocante*

-   because the girl is calling him
-   since the girl is calling him
-   while the girl is calling him
-   if the girl is calling him
-   **(**not**)** the girl is called by him
-   **(NOT) he called the girl**

An ablative absolute may also have objects, clauses, or phrases inside the clause. Typically the noun and participle will stand at the beginning and end of the clause, and it follows the same rules as other dependent clauses (it can interrupt an independent clause, but it can’t be interrupted by one):

-   puellā eum vocante (with the girl calling him)
-   puellā suam matrem vocante (with the girl calling her mother)

Most ablative absolutes start with nouns and end with participles, but that is a tendency, not a rule.

Dative: The most common uses of the dative, besides indirect object, are reference and special adjectives.

Dative of reference/interest There are a few related uses of the dative that show who is personally connected with a statement or fact.

-   It can indicate from whose perspective the statement is true *illī virō silva magna visa est* (“for that man, the forest seemed large”)
-   Especially with body parts, it can show a close connection, and often translated with the English possessive. *membra illī feminae* (“that woman’s limbs”)

Dative with special adjectives occurs with adjectives that would naturally take a to/for in English as well. Adjectives like easy, difficult, fitting, etc. often appear with a dative that explains to whom or in what way the adjective is easy, etc. Several of these are included in the chapter vocabulary.

function normalizeCu(text) {

    return text
        .toLowerCase()
        .normalize("NFD")
        .replace(/[\u0300-\u036f]/g, "")
        .replace(/[.,!?;:'"]/g, "")
        .replace(/\s+/g, " ")
        .trim();
}
