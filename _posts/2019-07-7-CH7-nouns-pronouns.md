In addition to verb conjugations, Latin nouns and adjectives have families called **declensions**. Also like verb conjugations, a noun’s declension has no bearing on its gender or meaning. The 5 declensions are simply groups, and words typically—but not always—follow predictable patterns within those groups.

### Principles of Grammar

Many of today’s languages (including English) began as an inflected language based on case endings. Although word order has replaced inflection, we still see remnants in how different words become plural (foot/feet, child/children, table/tables) and how we use pronouns to show possession or use in a phrase (they/them/their). For example, “them see us” would be incorrect because “them” is an object based on its ending.

#### Pronouns

You have seen some Latin pronouns (is/eum, ea/eam) already in this book. **Pronouns** are deceptive because native speakers tend to take them for granted; they simply replace nouns, but how they work may vary in different languages. Therefore you are encouraged to spend extra time and effort memorizing **ALL** these forms. If you had to pause and think about every pronoun you encounter or keep a pronoun dictionary handy to distinguish between “they” and “them,” it would be difficult to interact with anyone.

There are two significant differences between Latin and English pronouns.

1) because all nouns have grammatical gender in Latin, objects are referred to by their grammatical gender. If, for example, you are referring to a table (mensa, mensae, feminine), the Romans would call it *ea* or *eam* (she, her), even though we would say *it*.

2) Romans had many more third person pronouns than we do. English speakers refer to *this* (something near) or *that* (something further away), but the Romans had four different pronouns to use.

### Principles of Latin

Rather than worrying about how each pronoun gets translated (because sometimes the translation changes based on context), think about the relationship between all four pronouns. They can be visualized as concentric circles:

![Nested circles used to show how context changes meaning given the relationship between words](/assets/Chapter-7-circles.png "Nested circles used to show how context changes meaning given the relationship between words")

The smaller circle (*is, ea, id*) just represents the simplest of pronouns, like “he” or “him” or “it.” The next circle (*hic, haec, hoc*) can still mean “he,” him,” or “it,” but it is sometimes more emphatic, like “this,” or “these.” *Ille, illa, illud* is more emphatic still, and can sometimes connote distance like “that” and “those.” The largest circle (*iste, ista, istud*) is less common, and often connotes separation. In legal speeches, for example, it is usually used by one party (whose client might be referred to as *is* or *hic*) when referring to the other (obviously guilty) party—*iste*.

When you encounter these pronouns, then, know that they can all refer to a he, she, it, or they, but each one has a slightly different force.

Demonstrative Adjectives: Just like in English, many pronouns can also serve as adjectives. One might say “I see that,” or “I see that table.” Each of the pronouns you have learned in Latin can likewise appear as a demonstrative adjective. Like any adjective, it must agree with the noun it modifies in case, number, and gender.

Cases: You have already learned the subject case (nominative), object case (accusative), and a directional case (ablative). In this chapter you’ll learn the last two major cases. In coming chapters, you’ll see how all the cases can be used in multiple ways (not just for subjects and objects).

### Principles of Latin

Latin has 7 cases, but 5 are far more common (the last two will be introduced later):

| Nominative (names the noun) | Subject – the _chef_ cooks   Predicate Nominative – she is a _chef_ |
| --- | --- |
| Genitive (describes, classifies, or restricts the noun) | Possession – the _chef’s_ hat   Partitive – some o_f the chefs_   Description – a chef _of great fame_ |
| Dative (describes who/what benefits from an action or who/what the action affects) | Indirect object – give the book t_o the chef\*_   Interest – we cook _for the chef_ |
| Accusative (describes the goal, direction, or extent of a verb; indicates direct object of transitive verb; shows a length of time) | Direct object – We see the _chef_   Motion toward – Take this t_o the chef\*_   Duration – The chef cooks _for many hours_ |
| Ablative (describes motion from, location, and instrument, shows a specific time) | Motion – Move the knives _away from the chef_   Place – There is soup _on the chef_   Instrument – the chef chops _with his knife_   Specific time – the chef is busy _at this time_. |

\*Both the dative and accusative can sometimes be translated as *to the chef*. The Romans tended to prefer the accusative if movement were more important and dative if benefit were more important, but these occasionally overlapped. For example, in the phrase “Caesar sends the reinforcements to the battle,” both the movement and benefit are important, but the Romans would tend to use the accusative to show motion. Be flexible in translation.

This is not an exhaustive list; it just covers the basics. Every declension, however, has the same cases and case uses. So once you learn the cases, you’ll be on the way to identifying all possible uses of nouns and pronouns.

### Nouns

Once you’ve memorized pronouns, you’ll note that they share some endings with nouns.

| Masculine and Feminine Nouns | 1st Declension |  | 2nd Declension |  | 3rd Declension |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | **singular** | **plural** | **singular** | **plural** | **singular** | **plural** |
| Nominative | puella | puellae | locus | locī | mōs | mōrēs |
| Genitive | puellae | puellārum | locī | locōrum | mōris | mōrum |
| Dative | puellae | puellīs | locō | locīs | mōrī | mōribus |
| Accusative | puellam | puellās | locum | locōs | mōrem | mōrēs |
| Ablative | puellā | puellīs | locō | locīs | mōre | mōribus |

| Neuter Nouns | 2nd Declension |  | 3rd Declension |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | **singular** | **plural** | **singular** | **plural** |
| Nominative | regnum | regna | litus | litora |
| Genitive | regnī | regnōrum | litoris | litorum |
| Dative | regnō | regnīs | litorī | litoribus |
| Accusative | regnum | regna | litus | litora |
| Ablative | regnō | regnīs | litore | litoribus |

Note that you cannot determine a word’s declension by its nominative form. To do that, you must memorize the genitive singular. The ending of this word (-ae, ī, or -is) tells you the declension (1st, 2nd, or 3rd, respectively). The part of the word that remains after you identify the ending is called the **Noun Stem**.

For the noun:

puer, puerī (m): boy

-   Observe the genitive singular (always the second form in vocabulary): *puerī*
-   Identify the word as second declension *\-ī*
-   Note the noun stem: *puer-*

You must use the stem to form the inflected forms of the noun. This doesn’t make a big difference in some words (like puella/puellae, or regnum/regnī), but it does in most words (like ager/agrī, mēns/mentis, and litus/litoris).

A few other important details about the declensions:

-   Endings for masculine and feminine nouns of the same declension are typically the same.
-   Most first declension nouns are feminine and most second declension nouns are not (this isn’t a rule, though).
-   There are no 1st declension neuter nouns.
-   The only significant difference between M/F nouns and N nouns is the neuter law: all nominative and accusatives are the same if the word is neuter.

Pattern Recognition

-   Demonstratives can be pronouns (he, she, him, they, them, etc.) or adjectives (*this* person, *those* mountains, *that* guy). If the word is close to another word of the same gender, case, and number, it is likely and adjective. If it’s a pronoun, always identify the antecedent.
-   Antecedents typically come before the pronoun, but they do not have to. Compare the English sentence: We asked for her, but the manager was out of her office.
