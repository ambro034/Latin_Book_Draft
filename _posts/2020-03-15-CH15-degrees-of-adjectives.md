### Principles of English

Comparison: Adjectives come in three **degrees**. They are positive, comparative, and superlative. By “positive,” we do not mean “good,” just “baseline.” The positive degree is the form of adjective you’ve already learned: good, long, happy, sad. The comparative degree (naturally) compares an adjective to something else. This can appear in several forms in English: sadder; kind of sad; rather sad; etc. You do not always need to translate the comparative as *sadder* or *more sad*. The superlative degree is the strongest form of the adjective. It also can appear in several forms in English: very sad; terribly sad; saddest.^[\[4\]](#footnote-4)^

The future tense relates information that will happen at a later time. Typically the future tense is best translated as “will \_\_\_\_” (active) or “will be \_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed” (passive).

### Principles of Latin

Review 3^rd^ declension adjectives, especially two-ending adjectives like *omnis, omne* and *gravis, grave* (Chapter 13).

To form the comparative and superlative forms of adjectives, you must first find the stem. As usual, that always comes from the second word in the vocabulary. So for 1^st^/2^nd^ adjectives (e.g., *longus, longa, longum*: long/tall), the stem comes from the feminine singular (*long-*). For 3^rd^ adjectives (e.g., *tristis, triste*: sad), the stem comes from (in this case) the neuter (*trist-*). For other 3^rd^ declension adjectives (e.g., acer, acris, acre: sharp, fierce; potens (potentis): powerful), the stem still comes from the second form (*acr-* and *potent-*, respectively).

## Comparison

##### Comparative

To form the comparative, take the stem and create a 2 termination, 3^rd^ declension adjective. No matter what declension the positive degree (original adjective) is, its comparative will be third declension. The M/F ending will be –*ior*, and the neuter ending will be –*ius*.

for the adjective *longus, longa, longum*, the comparative is:

| Singular |  | Plural |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Masc/Fem | Neuter | Masc/Fem | Neuter |
| longior | longius | longiorēs | longiora |
| longioris | longioris | longiorum | longiorum |
| longiorī | longiorī | longioribus | longioribus |
| longiorem | longius | longiorēs | longiora |
| longiorī | longiore | longioribus | longioribus |

Note: unlike typical third declension adjectives, these do not have the i-stem, so you do not have forms like *longioria* or *longiorīs*.

##### Superlative

To form the superlative, take the stem and add *–issimus* (m), *\-issima* (f), *\-issimum* (n). No matter the declension of the positive degree, all superlatives are 1/2 declension adjectives.

| Singular |  |  | Plural |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Masc | Fem | Neut | Masc | Fem | Neut |
| longissimus | longissima | longissimum | longissimī | longissimae | longissima |
| longissimī | longissimae | longissimī | longissimōrum | longissimārum | longissimōrum |
| longissimō | longissimae | longissimō | longissimīs | longissimīs | longissimīs |
| longissimum | longissimam | longissimum | longissimōs | longissimās | longissima |
| longissimō | longissimā | longissimō | longissimīs | longissimīs | longissimīs |

Irregular comparatives: like in English, some commonly occurring adjectives have different stems in their comparative and superlative degrees than they do in the positive degree. In English, we have good, better, and best, and Latin too has irregular forms. Instead of memorizing them as a chart, the common irregular forms are simply included as part of the chapter vocabulary and are most easily memorized as vocabulary words.

##### ****Ablative of comparison****:

Sometimes in comparison, Latin puts the second noun or adjective in the ablative to show comparison:

*illa puella fortior est illō puerō.*

*illī puerī fortiorēs sunt illīs puellīs.*

##### ****Comparison with quam****:

More commonly, though, the coordinating conjunction *quam* is used, and the two words being compared stay in the same case:

*illa puella fortior est quam ille puer.*

*Caesar dicit se ambulāre celeriorem quam Lucium.*

## Future and Future Perfect Tense

In English, helping verbs “will” or “shall” are used to make a verb future tense. In Latin, the tense marker or a vowel change without a helping verb creates the future tense.

The rules vary slightly based on the conjugation. As is usual, the 1^st^ and 2^nd^ conjugation are very similar. For these, the tense indicator *–bi-* is used. Exceptions come from previously noted tendencies. The “i” is often contracted into the “o” in first person singular, and the Romans tended to avoid “int” as an ending.

##### Future Active (1^st^ and 2^nd^ conjugation)

|  | 1st conjugation singular | plural | 2nd conjugation singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | vocābō | vocābimus | vidēbō | vidēbimus |
| 2nd | vocābis | vocābitis | vidēbis | vidēbitis |
| 3rd | vocābit | vocābunt | vidēbit | vidēbunt |

##### Future active (3^rd^, 3^rd^ –io, 4^th^)

For 3^rd^, 3^rd^ –io, and 4^th^ conjugations, though, a vowel change occurs. The thematic vowel changes to “a” for 1^st^ person singular and “e” for the other persons/number. As usual, 3^rd^ –io verbs tend to behave like 4^th^, and both have an “i” before the new thematic vowel.

| 3rd conjugation | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | regam | regēmus |
| 2nd | regēs | regētis |
| 3rd | reget | regent |

| 4th and 3rd –io conjugations | singular | plural | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | audiam | audiēmus | capiam | capiēmus |
| 2nd | audīes | audiētis | capiēs | capiētis |
| 3rd | audiet | audient | capiet | capient |

Future passive (1^st^ and 2^nd^ conjugation)

The standard rules apply, with just a few phonetic variations.

| 1st and second conjugations | singular | plural | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | vocābor | vocābimur | vidēbor | vidēbimur |
| 2nd | **vocāberis** | vocābiminī | **vidēberis** | vidēbiminī |
| 3rd | vocābitur | vocābuntur | vidēbitur | vidēbuntur |

##### Future Passive (3^rd^, 3^rd^ –io, and 4^th^ conjugation)

For 3^rd^, 3^rd^ –io, and 4^th^, conjugations, though, a vowel change occurs. The thematic vowel changes to “a” for 1^st^ person singular and “e” for the other persons/number. As usual, 3^rd^ –io verbs tend to behave like 4^th^, and both have an “i” before the new thematic vowel.

| 3rd conjugation | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | regar | regēmur |
| 2nd | regēris | regēminī |
| 3rd | regetur | regentur |

| 4th and 3rd –io conjugations | singular | plural | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | audiar | audiēmur | capiar | capiēmur |
| 2nd | audiēris | audiēminī | capiēris | capiēminī |
| 3rd | audietur | audientur | capietur | capientur |

For the future tense of irregular verbs like sum and possum, see the **appendix**.

\*magis … quam introduces an adverbial comparison. Instead of a noun being taller, faster, heavier, etc., this construction compares to actions (e.g., “that horse runs *faster than* the dog;” not “that horse is *faster…*” For this construction, *magis* typically appears net to the basic word, while *quam* appears next to the comparative word. Word order varies, but the simplest way to translate is to put the English words “more than” between the two verbs (so, *ea longius magis videt is quam currere potest* = “she sees further than he is able to run”).
