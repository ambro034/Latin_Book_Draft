### Principles of Grammar

Students of Latin often have difficulty with the number of clauses that are in a typical sentence. Whereas English speakers and writers tend to use many independent clauses with (and sometimes without) coordinating conjunctions, the Classical Latin authors make more use of dependent clauses and subordinating conjunctions. A dependent clause still has a subject (what the clause is about) and predicate (the verb and what the subject is/does), but it does not express a complete thought, so it cannot stand on its own.

There are many types of dependent clauses, but observe just a few:

-   I heard *what you said*. (noun clause)
    -   subject (you)
    -   predicate (said)
    -   functions to replace a noun—the noise that the speaker heard
-   I’ll see you *when you get here*. (adverb clause)
    -   subject (you)
    -   predicate (get here)
    -   replaces an adverb—when the action happens

Because the two languages often differ in this respect, it can be challenging to reconcile Latin with an English translation. In order to achieve this, you must identify sentence segments (prepositional phrases, independent clauses, and especially dependent clauses). If you treat each segment like an integral part of the sentence, it becomes easier to understand the sentence as a whole. As you are reading, note that there is a rule found in most Latin, with little exception: ***dependent clauses can interrupt independent clauses, but they cannot be interrupted by independent clauses***. Furthermore, dependent clauses can interrupt other dependent clauses, but each must finish before the previous clause resumes. The rule exists in English too, we just tend to take it for granted.

Take for example the following sentence:

Caesar, when the troops who conquered Gaul arrived, was delighted.

The independent clause, *Caesar was delighted*, is interrupted by a dependent clause, *when the troops arrived*. That dependent clause is interrupted by another dependent clause, *who conquered Gaul*. By paying close attention to this simple structure, it becomes much easier to read lengthy Latin sentences. Likewise, the sentence would mean something completely different if it said “Caesar conquered the delighted troops.” It is important to keep segment separate to keep the translations accurate.

There are several types of dependent clauses, and many belong in the subjunctive mood (to be learned later). The two we will learn about in this chapter are *temporal clauses* and *relative clauses*.

-   Temporal clauses describe a relationship in time with the independent clause: *When he arrives, we will celebrate*. Or *Before he arrives, we will celebrate*.
-   Relative clauses generally describe what person or thing the main clause is about: *The leader who arrived first*. Or *We found the men who escaped*.

### Principles of Latin

Temporal clauses begin with a subordinating conjunction or adverb (for our purposes, we’ll just identify all of these as subordinating conjunctions).

The most common are *cum* (when), *antequam* (before), *postquam* (after), and *dum* (while). *Cum* can also be a preposition that takes the ablative (accompaniment), but context can help determine which part of speech it is. The subordinating conjunction *ubi* can mean “when” or “where,” depending on context.

These clauses can be joined to the beginning or end of an independent clause, or they can be inserted in the middle. As always, the temporal clause must end before the independent clause resumes.

Relative clauses typically begin with a relative pronoun—sometimes with a preposition and relative if necessary—that refers to another word within the main clause. Relative pronouns are translated as *which, that*, *who, whose,* or *whom*, depending on their use the case. The difference between which/that and who/whose/whom depends on the **antecedent**. This is the word that the pronoun replaces or renames. Personal pronouns also have antecedents, but relative pronouns typically refer to an antecedent in the independent clause. The relative must have the same gender and number as the antecedent, but its case depends on its function in the relative clause.

Examples:

-   locī quōs vidēmus in montibus iacent
    -   The places that we see lie in the mountains
    -   *loci* (places): masculine, plural; nominative subject of independent clause
    -   *quos* (that): masculine, plural; accusative object of relative clause
-   Via in quā ambulāmus longa est
    -   The road on which we walk is long
    -   via (roads): feminine, singular; nominative subject of independent clause
    -   *in qua* (on which): feminine singular; ablative (location) in relative clause
-   verba cognoscunt quae canimus
    -   They recognize the words that we sing
    -   *verba* (words): accusative object of independent clause
    -   *quae* (that): accusative object of relative clause
-   Puerī puellaeque quī legere possunt ā hōminibus amantur
    -   Boys and girls who are able to read are loved by people
    -   *pueri puellaeque* (boys and girls): nominative subject of independent clause
    -   *qui* (who): nominative subject of relative clause

The following chart lists the relative pronoun and its translations. Relative pronouns (like all pronouns) are ubiquitous, so it will be of great benefit to memorize every form and recall its use quickly. Notice that English does not distinguish between singular and plural:

| Singular | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Translations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | quī | quae | quod | who, which, what, that |
| Genitive | cuius | cuius | cuius | whose, of whom, of which |
| Dative | cui | cui | cui | to/for whom; to/for which |
| Accusative | quem | quam | quod | whom, which, that |
| Ablative | quō | quā | quō | by whom; by which |
| Plural | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Translations |
| Nominative | quī | quae | quae | who, which, what, that |
| Genitive | quōrum | quārum | quōrum | whose, of whom, of which |
| Dative | quibus | quibus | quibus | to/for whom; to/for which |
| Accusative | quōs | quās | quae | whom, which, that |
| Ablative | quibus | quibus | quibus | by whom; by which |

function normalizeRel(text) {
    return text
        .toLowerCase()
        .normalize("NFD")
        .replace(/[\u0300-\u036f]/g, "")
        .replace(/[.,!?;:'"]/g, "")
        .replace(/\s+/g, " ")
        .trim();
}

function updateRelDisplay(q) {

    relQuestions[q].tokens.forEach(function(token, j) {

        const el = document.getElementById(
            `rel-token-${q}-${j}`
        );

        el.classList.toggle(
            "rel-antecedent",
            relState[q].antecedent === j
        );

        el.classList.toggle(
            "rel-clause",
            relState[q].clause.has(j)
        );
    });
}

function sameSetRel(studentSet, correctArray) {

    if (studentSet.size !== correctArray.length) {
        return false;
    }

    return correctArray.every(function(x) {
        return studentSet.has(x);
    });
}

window.checkRelQuiz = function() {

    let score = 0;
    const possible = relQuestions.length * 3;

    relQuestions.forEach(function(q, i) {

        const feedback = [];

        if (relState[i].antecedent === q.antecedent) {
            score++;
            feedback.push(
                `<div class="rel-correct">✓ Antecedent correct.</div>`
            );
        } else {
            feedback.push(
                `<div class="rel-incorrect">✗ Check the antecedent.</div>`
            );
        }

        if (sameSetRel(relState[i].clause, q.clause)) {
            score++;
            feedback.push(
                `<div class="rel-correct">✓ Relative clause correct.</div>`
            );
        } else {
            feedback.push(
                `<div class="rel-incorrect">✗ Check the limits of the relative clause.</div>`
            );
        }

        const student =
            document.getElementById(`rel-answer-${i}`).value;

        const pronounCorrect =
            q.answers.some(function(answer) {
                return normalizeRel(student) === normalizeRel(answer);
            });

        if (pronounCorrect) {
            score++;
            feedback.push(
                `<div class="rel-correct">✓ Latin form correct.</div>`
            );
        } else {
            feedback.push(
                `<div class="rel-incorrect">
                    ✗ Correct Latin: <strong>${q.display}</strong>.
                </div>`
            );
        }

        feedback.push(
            `<div class="rel-note">${q.explanation}</div>`
        );

        document.getElementById(
            `rel-feedback-${i}`
        ).innerHTML = feedback.join("");
    });

    document.getElementById(
        "rel-score"
    ).textContent =
        `Score: ${score} / ${possible}`;
};

window.resetRelQuiz = function() {

    relQuestions.forEach(function(q, i) {
        relState[i].mode = "antecedent";
        relState[i].antecedent = null;
        relState[i].clause = new Set();
    });

    buildRelQuiz();

    document.getElementById(
        "rel-score"
    ).textContent = "";
};

buildRelQuiz();

})();

        const card =
            document.createElement("div");

        card.className =
            "seg12-question";

        let passageHTML = "[";

        q.words.forEach(function(word, j) {

            passageHTML +=
                `<span class="seg12-word">${word}</span>`;

            if (j < q.words.length - 1) {

                passageHTML += `
                    <button
                        type="button"
                        class="seg12-break"
                        id="seg12-break-${i}-${j}"
                        data-q="${i}"
                        data-after="${j}"
                        aria-label="Toggle segment boundary after ${word}">
                    </button>
                `;
            }
        });

        passageHTML += "]";

        card.innerHTML = `

            <div class="seg12-source">
                ${q.source}
            </div>

            <div class="seg12-passage">
                ${passageHTML}
            </div>

            <div
                id="seg12-feedback-${i}"
                class="seg12-feedback"
                aria-live="polite">
            </div>
        `;

        quiz.appendChild(card);
    });

    quiz.querySelectorAll(
        ".seg12-break"
    ).forEach(function(button) {

        button.addEventListener(
            "click",
            function() {

                const q =
                    Number(button.dataset.q);

                const after =
                    Number(button.dataset.after);

                if (
                    seg12State[q].has(after)
                ) {

                    seg12State[q].delete(after);

                    button.classList.remove(
                        "seg12-selected"
                    );

                } else {

                    seg12State[q].add(after);

                    button.classList.add(
                        "seg12-selected"
                    );
                }
            }
        );
    });
}

function sameSeg12Set(student, expected) {

    if (
        student.size !==
        expected.length
    ) {
        return false;
    }

    return expected.every(function(x) {
        return student.has(x);
    });
}

function modelSeg12(q) {

    return q.segments
        .map(function(segment) {
            return `[${segment}]`;
        })
        .join(" ");
}

## Chapter 12 Vocabulary

You will begin to learn Latin proper names in this chapter. Romans typically had three names. The first name (praenomen) was just a common name. In fact, there were only about sixteen names used with any frequency, and they were typically abbreviated. You’ll learn the name and abbreviation for some of these. The second name (nomen) was a family name. Some names were held in great honor and being born or adopted into one of these names could advance one’s career (e.g., Brutus, Scipio). The third name (cognomen) was typically a name granted to someone as an honor or characteristic.

For Gaius Iulius Caesar, then. Gaius (abbreviated C. because in early Roman times it was Caius) was a common name, Iulius was his family name, and Caesar was his nickname. Supposedly he was born by Caesarian section (caedo, caedere, cecidi, caesus, to cut). This story seems unlikely, though, and another author claims that he was born with a head full of hair (*caesaries*). All sources are uncertain, though, and it is also possible that he received the nickname from someone else in his family.

Proper nouns in Latin decline just like common nouns. Most masculine names were second or third declension; feminine names were often first declension versions of the family name. Thus females in the family of Gaius Iulius Caesar would usually just have been known to historians as Julia (a fact that makes it difficult for modern historians to figure out which Julia did what).

Because we do not decline proper nouns in English, we always translate inflected names as the nominative:

Caesar vocat = Caesar calls

arma Caesaris = Caesar’s weapons

vocat Caesarem = he calls Caesar
