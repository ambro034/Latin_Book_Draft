### Principles of Grammar

Pattern recognition: you have seen how word order in Latin is flexible; the endings of a word, not its position in the sentence, tell you its function (this is called **inflection**). You have learned two of these functions, and from now on we’ll also refer to them by their technical names: subject (**Nominative case**); and direct object (**Accusative case**).

In this chapter, we’ll begin to learn other functions. We’ll also discuss a new sentence pattern—**linking**.

Linking Verbs In addition to transitive (can take a direct object) verbs and intransitive (cannot take a direct object) verbs, linking verbs are widely used in Latin (and most languages). A linking verb does just what it says: it links a subject to a predicate. The most common linking verb in Latin and English is a form of “to be.” There is no direct object because no action is performed. Linking verbs do, however, take a **predicate nominative**. This is just a noun or adjective that is not the subject, but it is in the nominative (e.g., “the book is *long*,” or “the boat is a *yacht*”).

In Latin, the two linking verbs for this chapter are:

-   est = he/she/it is
-   sunt = they are

Prepositions these words typically identify relationships (mainly involving time or space) between words or clauses. For example, someone can come *before* the fourth day (time) or stand *before* the building (space). *Place* prepositions are far more common than *time* prepositions, so you’ll learn those first.

Although it doesn’t fit every preposition, there is an easy test to determine the types of words that are prepositions. Just plug them into the famous nursery rhyme: Hickory, dickory, dock. The mouse ran \_\_\_\_ the clock.

Declensions

You’ll note that different words have different sets of endings (for example, *puella*, *puellam*, and *puellā* all have a, but *mens*, *mentem*, and *mente* do not). They belong to different families of words. These families are called **declensions**. A declension is just a category that lets you memorize endings and find patterns more easily. There are five of these declensions, but you’ll only focus on three for a while.

### Principles of Latin

#### Neuter Nouns

The previous chapter introduced neuter adjective endings. In this chapter, you will learn some neuter nouns. As with any other noun, the word’s definition does not determine its gender; it is just part of the word’s meaning. With all neuter nouns, no matter the case, the nominative (subject case) and accusative (object case) always have the same endings. Context will help you determine its use in the sentence.

#### Location and motion Away (Ablative case)

So far you have seen two case uses of Latin nouns: subjects and direct objects. In this chapter, you will learn a new case and use—place. A more complete layout of these functions and their system will begin in chapter 7, but for now you can just familiarize yourself with the ending. The name applied to words with these endings is the **Ablative Case**

-   \-ā, -ō, -e (singular),
-   \-īs, -ibus (plural)

If a noun has one of these endings it will indicate either: 1) motion away from something; or 2) location (in or on something).

Examples:

-   They jumped *down from* the last step (motion away)
-   They ran *away from* the steps (motion away).
-   They remained *on* the last step (location).

In Latin, as in English, these spatial relationships typically follow the same patterns that are determined by prepositions. For example, the boy (*puer*) can stand in the field (*in agrō*) or walk away from the field (*de agrō*). In both examples, the ō at the end of the word shows that the word *field* in this category (not the subject or object category), and the preposition shows the exact relationship. Also like English, Latin prepositions often do a lot of heavy lifting, so one preposition can show several different relationships.

\***An important early tool for translation: remember that prepositions show relationships to words, and those relationships are typically different in every language**. Therefore, there is not often a 1:1 correspondence in translation. Sometimes the Latin preposition *in* can be translated the same as English (in), but in can also mean into, onto, against, among (and more). Learn what the words are doing in the sentence (this is called **syntax**), not simply what they would look like in English.

### Pattern Recognition

-   Although you’ll learn new uses for *est/sunt*, any time you see them for now will indicate a linking pattern.
-   Remember that when you see *ab, de,* or *ex*, there will be an ablative nearby. Anything between the preposition and the ablative belongs inside the segment when you translate. Later, you’ll learn more prepositions that go with the Ablative, and some that go with the Accusative case.

| **Verbs** | *intransitive verbs are marked with an asterisk |
| it / eunt* | he/she/it goes, they go |
| est / sunt | he/she/it is, exists; they are, exist |
| ambulat / ambulant* | he/she/it walks; they walk |
| **Pronouns** |  |
| id (singular) ea (plural) | it, them (neuter pronoun) |
| **Adverbs** |  |
| -nē (enclitic)* | _creates a yes/no question_ |
| bene | well |

*\*-ne* can be added to most sentences, usually (but not always) to the first word or the verb, to create a question. The Romans did not have question marks, so this was their way of signaling a question. The question can only be a “magic 8-ball” question though: “do they know the truth?”; not “what is the truth?”
