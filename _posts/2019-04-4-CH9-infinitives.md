### Principles of Grammar

The infinitive, or “not finite” form of the verb, is typically translated as “to \_\_\_” (*to go*, *to run*). It is the most basic form of the verb and does not have personal endings (hence its names). So far, we have just used the infinitive to find the present stem, but the infinitive itself appears in many forms. Most of those forms correspond to English uses of the infinitive (one use, the **indirect statement**, is not like English; you’ll learn that in a later chapter).

In reading chapters, you have seen several words that often go with infinitives. Words like “he is able” (*potest*) or “they want” (*volunt*) are accompanied by an infinitive, just like they are in English: “he is able *to walk*” or “they want *to dance*.”

The Ablative case, in addition to showing location and motion away, has many uses in Latin, most of them can be translated by the English preposition “with” or “from.” The two uses in this chapter can both be translated “with,” but they are very different constructions.

### Principles of Latin

  
Two uses of the infinitive correspond very closely with English. The 4th will be introduced in a later chapter. With all these uses, sentence patterns, especially a finite verb in the same clause, will help you recognize what type of infinitive you see.

infinitive with Modal Verb: an infinitive with this type of verb is also called **complementary** because its use “completes” the meaning of another verb. **Modal verbs** include *can (be able), want, wish, opt, prefer, dare*, all of which need an infinitive (“I want to walk” or “He dares to arrive late”).

  
Pattern recognition—Complementary infinitives will often appear in clauses that have verbs like *possum* (I am able), *debeo* (I ought), and *volo* (I want).  
*possumus ambulāre*: we are able to walk / we can walk  
*debent canere*: they ought to sing

*volo regere*: I want to rule

Infinitive as a Subject: The infinitive can function as a nominative, neuter, singular noun and can therefore serve as the subject. This allows the action to be described or to perform an action itself

Pattern recognition—Infinitives used as nouns will be the subject; other sentence patterns like transitive, intransitive, and linking, can still be present. There will not typically be a verb that depends on an infinitive (like *possum*), unless it is dependent on another infinitive that takes a complementary (and thus combines both patterns:  
*legere bonum est*: reading is good  
*iacēre eum servat*: lying down protects him

*legere posse bonum est*: being able to read is good

Ablative of Means/Instrument: the literal or figurative means by which something is accomplished. We write *with our pens*. We love *with all our hearts*. He defeated the enemy *with his sword*.

Pattern recognition—To express the means or instruments by which something happens, the Romans typically used the ablative **without** a preposition (means/instrument):

vidēmus oculīs: we see with our eyes

homō nōs beneficiō iūvit: the man pleased us with kindness

Ablative of Accompaniment: shows the people or animals participating in the clause. We write *with our friends*. He fought *with the enemy*.

Pattern recognition—To express the person (or animals) that participate in an action, the Romans typically used the ablative with the preposition *cum* (accompaniment):

cum multīs equīs ambulāmus: we walk with many horses

canimus cum coniugibus: we sing with (our) spouses

Other personal pronouns:

is/ea/id is the 3rd person pronoun, but Latin also has 1st and 2nd person pronouns. The nominative form of these isn’t generally necessary. *ego amō* and *amō* both mean “I love,” but the pronoun can either show emphasis or signal a change in the grammatical subject.

Personal pronouns do not decline like any other nouns, but they all follow a similar pattern:

| 1st person | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | ego (I) | nōs (we) |
| Genitive | meī (of me) | nostrum/nostrī* (of us) |
| Dative | mihi (to/for me) | nōbīs (to us) |
| Accusative | mē (me) | nōs (us) |
| Ablative | mē (with**/ from me) | nōbīs (with**/from us) |

| 2nd person | Singular | Plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | tū | vōs |
| Genitive | tuī | vestrum/vestrī* |
| Dative | tibi | vōbīs |
| Accusative | tē | vōs |
| Ablative | tē** | vōbīs** |

Notes:

\*The genitive plural for *nōs* and *vōs* have two forms. This just depended on which type of genitive the Romans used (“some *of us* are here” would be *nostrum*, but “our parents are proud *of us*” would be *nostri*).

\*\*To form the ablative of accompaniment with personal pronouns, the Romans put the preposition onto the end of the word. Instead of *cum me*, the Romans said *mēcum* (with me), *nobiscum* (with us), *tēcum* (with you), and *vobiscum* (with y’all).

3rd -io verbs The last regular conjugation of verbs is the 3rd -io. These are 3rd conjugation (their infinitive ends with -ere), but they often behave like 4th conjugations (including in their first principal part, which ends in -io instead of -o). faciō, facere, fēcī, factus (to make, to do) is a common 3rd -io. As with 4th conjugation verbs, all forms of the present stem need an *\-i-* (but for 3^rd^ –io verbs, it is short). The perfect system (as with all Latin verbs) is regular:

| present | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st person | faciō | facimus |
| 2nd person | facis | facitis |
| 3rd person | facit | faciunt |

| imperfect | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st person | faciēbam | faciēbāmus |
| 2nd person | faciēbās | faciēbātis |
| 3rd person | faciēbat | faciēbant |

| perfect | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st person | fēcī | fēcīmus |
| 2nd person | fēcistī | fēcistis |
| 3rd person | fēcit | fēcērunt / fēcēre |

\*possum: this verb is a compound of *pot-* (power, ability) and *sum* (to be). If the form of sum starts with an *s*, then the stem **assimilates** to *pos-* (e.g., *possum, possunt*). If the form of sum starts with an e, the stem remains *pot-* (e.g., *potes, poteras*).

\*\*Many verbs have multiple compound forms. For example, a prefix can be attached to capio to form: accipio (to receive); excipio (to remove); etc.

By memorizing the common Latin prefixes, you’ll be able to learn multiple words just by learning the stem.

\*\*\*For now, this irregular verb will only be used in 1^st^ singular, 3^rd^ singular, and 3^rd^ plural forms.

These are the most common prefixes:

ab/a/abs: away

ad: toward \*note that the “d” in this prefix often changes to match the first letter of the stem verb (e.g., accipio). This is called **assimilation**. Likewise, if this happens, an *a* or *e* in the infinitive stem will change to an *i* (so *capere* becomes *accipere*)

con/com: with, together, very (this prefix comes from the preposition cum; the final consonant also assimilates).

dis: apart

en/em/in: into, against, not

ex, e, ef: out, from, completely

ob: toward, completely

per: through, completely

prae: before, in front of

re: back/again

sub: under (the final b often assimilates)
