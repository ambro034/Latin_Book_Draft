### Principles of Grammar

The subjunctive is often used for adverb clauses and noun clauses. In this chapter, you will learn the most common adverb clauses. An **adverb clause** is, as strange as it may sound, a clause that functions as an adverb: it modifies a verb in the main clause or another dependent clause. Here is an example:

The storm came quickly.

-   In this sentence, *quickly* is an adverb that describes the verb *came*. But the sentence can be more descriptive:

The storm came so quickly that we hid under a rock.

-   In this sentence, *so quickly that we hid under a rock* is still modifying the verb *came*, but it is also a dependent clause.

Adverbs clauses describe the purpose, result, or circumstance of a verb. When this happens, Latin typically uses the subjunctive, but when we translate to English the verb is often the same as the indicative. The pattern will indicate the correct translation.

There are four main adverbial clauses: purpose, result, *cum* clauses, and conditions. Each one uses the subjunctive, and (for the most part) they have specific markers to show you which pattern to use.

-   Purpose clauses answer the question: “why did the subject perform the action?” (e.g., “I study *so that I may pass the test*”)
-   Result clauses explain the outcome of an action (e.g., “I studied so hard *that I passed the test*”)
-   Cum clauses (e.g., “*Because I studied hard,* I passed the test”)
-   conditions (e.g., “*If I had studied hard, I would have passed the test*”)

### Principles of Latin

Adverb clauses use a subordinating conjunction (you learned about these with *cum* and relative clauses in chapter 12). For these clauses, though, the verb is in the subjunctive. Finding the pattern will help you translate the clause quickly and accurately.

-   Purpose clauses are typically marked with the conjunction *ut* or *ne* (*ne* takes the place of *non* in some subjunctive clauses).
    -   consul scribit *ut mutent* (the consul writes *so that they may change*)
    -   consul scribit *ne mutent* (the consul writes *so that they may not change*)
-   Result clauses are typically marked with the conjunction *ut* or *ut non*. This clause will usually (but not always) have a signal word (*ita*\-so, in such a way; *tantus*\-so much) in the main clause that will signal a result clause. A comprehensive list of these words is listed in the chapter vocabulary.
    -   consul **ita** scribit *ut mutent* (the consul writes in such a way *that they change*)
    -   consul **ita** scribit *ut non mutent* (the consul writes in such a way *that they do not change*)
-   Cum clauses start with the subordinating conjunction *cum* and show a relationship between the main clause and the dependent clause. There are three types of relationships, outlined below
-   Conditionals show an “if...then...” relationship between the main clause and the dependent clause. They include the subordinating conjunction *si* (if), but can be translated in a variety of ways outlined below.

##### Purpose Clause

These are the simplest and most common adverb clauses. They are marked by *ut* or *ne*.

\*Patterns: Note that if the verb in the main clause is present, future, or (sometimes perfect—translate “has \_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed” or “have \_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed”) the subjunctive will be present or perfect. If the verb is any other tense (and sometimes perfect—translate “\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed”), the subjunctive verb will be imperfect or pluperfect.

##### Result clause

These clauses will almost always have an adverb (*ita, sic, tam, adeo, talis, tantus, tot, totiens*) in the main clause to signal a consequence. They can be translated with the old, but mean middle school “your mom” joke (“your mom is *so* ugly, that she threw a boomerang and it refused to come back”). The adverb does not have to be there, but it will typically be used in this text so you can identify the pattern.

##### Cum clause

You have already learned one *cum* clause that uses an indicative verb. It’s called a temporal clause (*cum viam vident ambulabunt* “when they see the road they will walk”). There are three types of *cum* clauses that use subjunctive verbs. Context is the best way to decide how to translate, but sometimes there is room for interpretation.

-   Circumstantial clauses: these are the most common and most basic (*cum viam videant ambulant* “when they see the road, they walk”).
-   Causal clauses: context and your own interpretation will help, but these show a causal relationship between the two clauses (*cum viam videant ambulant* “because they see the road, they walk”).
-   Adversative clauses: occur when the independent clause shows something that happened despite the dependent clause (*cum viam videant ambulant* “even though they see the road, they walk”). These clauses often use the particle *tamen* in the dependent clause (*cum viam videant tamen ambulant*) to make sure the reader understands the pattern.

##### Conditions

These clauses require the most attention to patterns. Each clause has a subjunctive or indicative verb, and the tense and voice of the verb show you how to translate. We’ll start with 6 basic patterns. Each pattern has two clauses. The dependent clause (also called the **protasis**) is the “if” part of the condition and is started with *si* “if” or *nisi* “unless”. The independent clause (**apodosis) is the “then” part of the condition, and does not have any clause markers.**

| **indicative (real)** | protasis | apodosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| present general | present indicative | present indicative |
| past general | perfect indicative | perfect indicative |
| future more-vivid | future indicative | future indicative |
| **subjunctive (unreal)** |  |  |
| future less-vivid (should/would) | present subjunctive | present subjunctive |
| present contrafactual | imperfect subjunctive | imperfect subjunctive |
| past contrafactual | pluperfect subjunctive | pluperfect subjunctive |

Real:

-   present general: *si senātus regit, fallīmur*: If the senate rules, we are deceived.
-   past general: *si senātus rēxit, falsī sumus*: If the senate ruled, we were deceived.
-   future more-vivid: *si senātus reget, fallēmur*. If the senate (will) rule, we will be deceived.

Unreal:

-   future less-vivid (should/would): *si id aedificias, ei veniant*: Should you build it, they would come.
-   present contrafactual: *si id aedificeres, ei venirent*: Were you building it, they would come.
-   past contrafactual: *si id aedifecisses, ei vēnissent*: Had you built it, they would have come.

Many conditions you see in Latin are a combination (a perfect subjunctive in the protasis and a present indicative in the apodosis. Just remember the translation rule for each part of the condition and combine.
