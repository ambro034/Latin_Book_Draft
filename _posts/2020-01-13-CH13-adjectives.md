### Principles of Grammar

Participles are commonly used but not always understood. A participle, by definition, is a verbal adjective. Like a verb, it has some verbal elements (tense, voice). Since it is also an adjective, it has to agree with a noun in case, number, and gender (see also: **predicative participles**). Participles, like infinitive verbs have tense relative (prior, simultaneous, oe subsequent) to the main verb, not absolute time (perfect, present, future).

Compare two sentences:

*The* ***fast*** *horse ran toward the* ***tall*** *gate*

*The galloping horse ran toward the broken gate*

In the sentence *The fast horse ran toward the tall gate*, the adjectives *fast* and *tall* modify the nouns *horse* and *gate*, respectively. We know that the horse is fast and the gate tall because of word order.

If we replace the adjectives with participles, we would get something like: *The galloping horse ran toward the broken gate*. *Galloping* and *broken* both come from verbs (to gallop, to break), but now they describe nouns, and are therefore verbs that come from adjectives (verbal adjectives = participles).

Note too the verbal elements of participles. The first, *galloping*, is present, because it happens at the same time as *ran*. It is also active, because the horse is performing the action. *Broken*, on the other hand, implies that the gate has already been broken (complete action), so it is a perfect participle. Likewise, the gate received that action, so it is passive.

Note: English uses participles and helping verbs to create different tenses and voices, so pay attention to what a word is doing, not just what it looks like. We have seen this in the formation of imperfect (I was calling) and passive (I was called). The difference between *the galloping horse* and *the horse was galloping* is small, but significant. One is a clause, the other is not.

### Principles of Latin

Like English, Latin has a present active participle and a perfect passive participle. Latin also has future participles that will be introduced later.

Present Active participle: To form the present active participle, start with the present stem (since that gives you everything you need for the present system). If you add –ns to the nominative and –ntis to the genitive, you’ll have a third declension adjective (also introduced in this chapter) that forms the present participle. 4^th^ and 3^rd^ –io verbs add an *i* to the present stem.

Perfect passive participle: is simply the fourth principal part, but declined like magnus, magna, magnum. Latin does not have a present passive or perfect active participle, so there is no way to say “*being broken*” or “*having ran*.” \*see **deponent verbs** below\*

Adjectives: In addition to “verbal” adjectives, there are a few new types of adjectives to learn. Like all adjectives, they agree with a noun in case, number, and gender, but they do not look exactly like the bonus, bona, bonum type adjective we’ve seen so far.

–ius adjectives: These nine adjectives, most of them pertaining to quantity (one, another, neither), look like a typical 1/2 adjective, but the genitive and dative singular forms are the same as demonstratives like *ille*. They are otherwise like the other adjectives you have learned so far. They are most commonly remembered by the acrostic **UNUS NAUTA:**

-   Ūnus, ūna, ūnum: one
-   Nūllus, nūlla, nūllum: none
-   Uter, utra, utrum: either
-   Sōlus, sōla, sōlum: alone
-   Neuter, neutra, neutrum: neither
-   Alter, altera, alterum: one (of two) \* often appears twice (the one…., the other….)
-   Ūllus, ūlla, ūllum: any
-   Tōtus, tōta, tōtum: the whole, entire
-   Alius, alia, aliud: other

*Unus*, of course, only has a singular form. The others can be declined like this:

| Nominative | nullus | nulla | nullum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Genitive | nullius | nullius | nullius |
| Dative | nullī | nullī | nullī |
| Accusative | nullum | nullam | nullum |
| Ablative | nullō | nullā | nullō |

Plural:

| Nominative | nullī | nullae | nulla |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Genitive | nullōrum | nullārum | nullōrum |
| Dative | nullīs | nullīs | nullīs |
| Accusative | nullōs | nullās | nulla |
| Ablative | nullīs | nullīs | nullīs |

As you can see, this is just like the declension of *ille, illa, illud*. The others follow the same pattern.

Possessive Adjectives**:** These are closely related to the personal pronouns. Remember to think of patterns: pronouns refer to nouns (antecedents); adjectives describe nouns. Words like I, we, us, and them are pronouns, while words like my, mine, ours, yours are adjectives. All of these adjectives decline like bonus, bona, bonum. *Meus, mea, meum* has been done as an example for you.

Meus, mea, meum: my/mine

Tuus, tua, tuum: your/yours

Noster, nostra, nostrum: our/ours

Vester, vestra, vestrum: y’alls

| Dative | meō | meae | meō |

| Nominative | meī | meae | mea |

Like all adjectives, possessives have two parts: the stem gives the meaning (*mine, ours, yours*), while the ending agrees with a noun in case, number, and gender. This means that the gender of “mine” or “my” has nothing to do with the gender of the possessor, only what the adjective modifies. For example, in the *Vulgate*—the Latin translation of the Bible—Jesus says to his disciples during the last supper: *accipite, comoedite; hoc est corpus meum* (take and eat, this is my body). Both *hoc* and the possessive adjective *meum* are all neuter simply because the grammatical gender of *corpus* is neuter. As a fun side note, the notion of bread changing into the body of Christ led to the phrase “*hoc est corpus*” changed into “hokus pokus” when medieval magicians performed their acts.

Reflexive Possessive Adjectives: In addition to using the genitive of possession for 3rd person pronouns (*eius, eōrum, eārum*, and similar), the Romans had a second type of possessive related to the reflexive pronoun learned previously. This difference, which is often unclear in English, is helpful for communication.

In the sentence “Titus and Brutus walked in a field, and Titus saw his horse,” it can be unclear whether Titus saw his own horse or the horse of Brutus. This difficulty does not exist in Latin, which has two different words for the “his” used above.

**Suus, sua, suum** is the reflexive possessive adjective. It works just like the adjectives above, but it refers back to the subject as the possessor. Thus:

Titus Brutusque in agrō ambulāvērunt.

Titus suum equum videt = Titus sees his own horse.  
Titus eius equum videt = Titus sees his (someone else’s) horse.

3rd Declension adjeCtives: The last of the new adjectives are from a different family, but get their endings (for the most part) from the 3^rd^ declension nouns you’ve already learned. For these, endings for the genitive plural, nominative and accusative plural, and the ablative singular are slightly different. As long as you can recognize the difference (especially the ablative singular), there’s no need to memorize these as a separate group.

-   the ablative singular (all genders) is *–ī* instead of *–e*
-   the genitive plural (all genders) is *–ium* instead of *–um*
-   the nominative and accusative plural (neuter) is *–ia* instead of *–a*

There is one slight difference. 1^st^/2^nd^ declension adjectives all have different endings for masculine, feminine, and neuter. 3^rd^ declension adjectives sometimes follows this (3-ending), sometimes the masculine and feminine endings are the same (2-ending), and sometimes the ending for all three genders is the same (3-ending). This is just something to remember in vocabulary, but the pattern for all three is below:

| Genitive | acris | acris | acris | acrium | acrium | acrium |

Formation of Participles: The present active participle comes from the present stem:

**Present active**

| infinitive | present active participle |
| --- | --- |
| 1st: vocāre (to call) | vocans, vocantis (calling) |
| 2nd: vidēre (to see) | vidēns, videntis (seeing) |
| 3rd: regere (to rule) | regēns, regentis (ruling) |
| 3rd –io: capere (to take) | capiēns, capientis (taking) |
| 4th: audīre (to hear) | audiēns, audientis (hearing) |

The present active participle likewise declines like a 1-ending 3^rd^ declension adjective so that it can agree in gender, number, and case with the noun it modifies:

| Masculine and Feminine |  | Neuter |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| vocāns | vocantēs | vocāns | **vocantia** |
| vocantis | **vocantium** | vocantis | **vocantium** |
| vocanti | vocantibus | vocantī | vocantibus |
| vocantem | **vocantīs** | vocans | **vocantia** |
| **vocantī** | vocantibus | vocante | vocantibus |

**Perfect Passive** (Note that everything in the perfect system is regular, so all conjugations and irregular verbs will have the same endings)

vocātus, vocāta, vocātum: called; having been called

| Singular |  |  | Plural |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
| vocātus | vocāta | vocātum | vocātī | vocātae | vocāta |
| vocātī | vocātae | vocātī | vocātōrum | vocātārum | vocātōrum |
| vocātō | vocātae | vocātō | vocātīs | vocātīs | vocātis |
| vocātum | vocātam | vocātum | vocātōs | vocātās | vocāta |
| vocātō | vocātā | vocātō | vocātīs | vocātīs | vocātīs |

Unlike English, most—but not all—participles modify whole clauses rather than individual nouns (see **attributive** and **predicative** participles in the index).

The sentence below can be translated several ways, depending on context:

Equus a multīs **petitus** ad viam currit. The horse, sought by many, runs toward the road.

The horse that was sought by many runs toward the road.

Because the horse was sought by many it ran toward the road.

The horse, when it was sought by many, ran toward the road.

In the first example, the perfect participle *petitus* is taken to modify the noun horse. In the other examples, although the participle agrees with *equus*, it is taken to modify the dependent clause.

Caesar, illōs **ducēns**, Romam relinquit. Caesar, leading those men, leaves Rome.

Caesar, who is leading those men, leaves Rome.

While Caesar is leading those men, he leaves Rome.

Although it’s difficult to choose the right sense of the participle when translating individual sentences, context will make it easier once you read them in passages.

Segmenting: Participles can mark sentence segments just like verbs, punctuation, conjunctions, and prepositions. Participles are often used to govern an independent clause, so they will contain the same place/time/means constructions as the verbs from which they come.

        .toLowerCase()
        .normalize("NFD")

        .replace(/\s+/g, " ")

function normalizeVp(text) {

    return text
        .toLowerCase()
        .normalize("NFD")
        .replace(/[\u0300-\u036f]/g, "")
        .replace(/[.,!?;:'"]/g, "")
        .replace(/\s+/g, " ")
        .trim();
}

function buildVpQuiz() {

    const quiz =

                    "vp-selected"
                );

                    "true"

window.checkVpQuiz = function() {

    let score = 0;
    const possible =
        vpQuestions.length * 2;

    vpQuestions.forEach(function(q, i) {

        const feedback = [];

        const selected =
            document.querySelector(
                `.vp-choice.vp-selected[data-q="${i}"]`
            );

        if (
            selected &&
            selected.dataset.value === q.type
        ) {

            score++;

            feedback.push(
                `<div class="vp-correct">
                    ✓ ${q.type} is correct.
                </div>`
            );

        } else {

            feedback.push(
                `<div class="vp-incorrect">
                    ✗ This phrase requires:
                    <strong>${q.type}</strong>.
                </div>`
            );
        }

        const student =
            document.getElementById(
                `vp-answer-${i}`
            ).value;

        const translationCorrect =
            q.answers.some(function(answer) {

                return normalizeVp(student) ===
                       normalizeVp(answer);

            });

        if (translationCorrect) {

            score++;

            feedback.push(
                `<div class="vp-correct">
                    ✓ Latin translation correct.
                </div>`
            );

        } else {

            feedback.push(
                `<div class="vp-incorrect">
                    ✗ One correct form is:
                    <strong>${q.display}</strong>.
                </div>`
            );
        }

        if (q.note) {

            feedback.push(
                `<div class="vp-note">
                    ${q.note}
                </div>`
            );
        }

        document.getElementById(
            `vp-feedback-${i}`
        ).innerHTML =
            feedback.join("");
    });

    document.getElementById(
        "vp-score"
    ).textContent =
        `Score: ${score} / ${possible}`;
};

Like other verbs we’ve learned (e.g., capere), mitto has many compounds. They all follow the same principal part pattern as mitto, except they have prefixes. The following compounds should be memorized as part of the vocabulary: *admittere*, to urge on, to receive; *amittere*: to lose, dismiss; *committere*, to unite, connect, engage; *demittere*: to drop, hang down, flow down; *permittere*: to allow, let through)
