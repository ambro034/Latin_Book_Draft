This chapter explains the goals, trajectory, and foundational material for the textbook, and it is integral to the approach for students and instructors.

### Purpose:

This book is the result of a project to modernize the way Latin is presented in the classroom. Its mission is to retain and rely upon the virtues of a grammar-based education model supplemented by the reading-based model and the strengths of modern language textbooks. The book is thereby based on the following basic principles:

-   **Pattern recognition** — A major strength of modern language textbooks is their acknowledgement that many students do not start the semester with a usable knowledge of grammar (English or Latin). The typical approach for ancient language instruction is to teach the grammatical labels and terms first. In this book the approach is different and more usefull for students. With each new concept, we do not focus on labels and technical terminology until the concept is introduced and reinforced. The primary focus is pattern recognition. Most students can identify the feeling of walking into a room, seeing certain characteristics and décor of that room, and then knowing what type of situation they are walking into (dorm, study group, basketball tournament watching party, etc.). The aim of this book is to help instructors and students teach the same recognition with Latin sentences: is this a transitive or intransitive sentence? Is this a sentence that requires a special verb? At the beginning the goal is pattern recognition at the expense of colloquial speech. Over time, students will learn the patterns of grammar and vocabulary, and therefore be able to read any sentence pattern with just a little vocabulary help. This inverts the old approach of building vocabulary and terminology first, which can prove frustrating for the instructor and student. The reward for pattern recognition pays off with the structure of each chapter.
-   **Anchoring** — One of the biggest challenges in Latin is the flood of new material. Students are asked to learn a new syntax, new vocabulary, and a new structure all at once. The second major approach of this book is to anchor students in what they know before moving on to new information. For that reason, some information will be presented in a different order than standard grammatical textbooks. Core grammatical ideas like dependent clauses, passive voice, participles, and use of infinitives are introduced early so students will internalize fundamental grammatical principles, then they will have them as anchors for new concepts. For example, the future tense, although similar in form to present and imperfect, is introduced much later than usual, since it is rare in most Latin texts and does not anchor any additional concepts.
-   **Statistical and semantic approach to vocabulary** — Only 30 of the 500 most common words in classical Latin are first declension nouns, and 48 are from the second declension. Conversely 87 are from the third. It does not make sense then to teach the first declension paradigm early on, when through the 3rd declension students can learn about noun gender, stem changes, and more common vocabulary. Similar distinctions can be found in the verb conjugations: 1st conjugation is just not common enough to be introduced first. This book introduced the first 3 declensions and first 2 conjugations together. Although fourth and fifth declensions are not introduced until the second semester, all conjugations are rapidly introduced. This book also groups particular families of vocabulary terms (earth/land terms; government; household; etc), in a way similar to what most modern language books use.
-   **Statistical approach to grammar** — Cases are introduced in order of importance, and importance equates to frequency. Many grammatical ideas will be delayed or omitted if they are not statistically relevant. This allows the case use to become an anchor before the paradigm is memorized. Similar approaches are n with verbs and adjectives.
-   **Sentences and stories** — Some of the practice sentences, especially in the first few chapters and in chapter exercises, are unrelated and designed to help introduce grammatical points. After chapter 3, short passages adapted from Latin texts or stories will reinforce previous concepts and anticipate future ones.
-   This book is written with the idea that although many students do not plan to become classics majors, all students do need a more nuanced approach to grammar, vocabulary, and communication in general. Significant literary, cultural, and historical information is therefore put aside or placed in an appendix so the student can focus on the primary goal of pattern recognition and language development. In classroom testing, chapters 1-10 are introduced in the first semester, chapters 11-19 in the second, and chapters 20 through the end are included for a possible third semester.  
    

### About Latin

Learning Latin is like learning to restore an old car that’s sitting in your garage. Although you may never use it daily, the lessons you learn will help you with future repairs and improvements that are more urgent. If you learn about the ancestors of our ancestors’ culture by understanding it, tinkering with it, and exploring, it will make other languages, communication in general, cultural interaction, and history come alive in new ways.
