### Principles of Grammar

The next two chapters introduce several less typical patterns and grammatical functions of verbs.

Aside from the future tense of finite verbs, there are also future tense participles and infinitives. Like their present and perfect counterparts, the future infinitives typically show time relative to the main verb. Just like perfect infinitives show action *prior* *to* the verb and present infinitives show action *the same time as* the main verbs, future forms show action *subsequent to* the main verb. The practicalities on how to translate future forms are in the examples below.

You’ve already seen that verbs can be used to form adjectives (participles). In this chapter you’ll also learn about nouns that can be formed from verbs: **gerunds**.

##### Future Participles

The future active participle is not terribly common in Classical Latin. It can serve as a replacement for future tenses or to be more emphatic (*vir nōn tentūrus urbem* “a man *not about to hold* the city”), and it also appears in later poetry.

The future passive participle (also called the **gerundive**) is more common. It is also noteworty in that it has additional uses beyond the other participles you have learned. The gerundive, which seems like it should be translated “will be \_\_\_\_\_ed,” more often connotes need or obligation (“should be \_\_\_\_\_ed”). This can be through a variety of uses but is often seen in a special construction called the **passive periphrastic**.

##### Infinitives

###### Infinitive as a noun

A use of the infinitive that does not require another, finite, verb (like complementary or indirect statement) is the **infinitive as a noun**. In this construction, the infinitive can only serve as a singular subject. It can be translated either (e.g.,) *to call* or *calling*. When the infinitive is used as a noun, is form is always neuter singular.

-   *To see* the countryside is my dream.
-   Seeing the countryside is relaxing.

Note that the future passive infinitive is rare and does not need to be memorized at this point. The future active infinitive is used almost exclusively with indirect statement. Since the translation of the infinitive is relative to the tense of the main verb in this construction, its translation will vary:

-   Present tense main verb: Caesar dicit eam vocātūram esse = Caesar *says* that she *will* call.
-   Imperfect/perfect tense main verb: Caesar dīxit eam vocātūram esse = Caesar *said* that she *would* call.

##### Gerunds

The gerundive (gerund*ive* = adject*ive*) is related to another type of word called the gerund. The gerundive (a participle) is a verbal adjective, while the gerund is a verbal noun. The noun is usually expressed in English with the suffix -ing. For example, I learn best *by reading*. (Note well: this is different from the participial phrase The person *reading* this book will learn.) The gerund is related to the new form of the infinitive introduced in this chapter, the infinitive as a noun. The infinitive as a noun can only be the subject of a sentence, so the gerund supplies the other cases:

-   Nominative: *To win (winning)* feels great (infinitive as a noun)
-   Genitive: A desire *of winning* makes me work hard
-   Dative: This type of practice is good for *winning*
-   Accusative: Let’s go there *to win* (accusative gerunds only exist with the prepositions *ad* or *in*)
-   Ablative: We earned praise from everyone *by winning* the tournament

### Principles of Latin

##### Participle Formation:

You have already learned the present and perfect participles. The future active participle is very similar to the future active infinitive:

| Participles | Active | Passive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Present | vocāns, vocāntis   vidēns, videntis   agēns, agentis   capiēns, capientis   audiēns, audientis | --- |
| Perfect | --- | vocātus, -a, -um |
| Future | vocātūrus, -a, -um | vocāndus, -a, -um   videndus, -a, -um   agendus, -a, -um   capiendus, -a, -um   audiendus, -a, -um |

The future passive participle also give us the form of gerunds and gerundives (both words are in fact formed from the future passive participle of gerere).

The gerundive is formed from the present stem. The ending *\-undus, -unda, -undum* is added to form a 1^st^/2^nd^ declension adjectives (just like bonus, bona, bonum). Translations of the gerundive vary (see below), but they often have the sense of necessity. Consider common derivatives of gerundives: agenda (from *agere*)—items that should be done; referendum (from *referre*)—a vote taken back to the entire electorate; legend (from *legere*)—a book/movie that should be read; Amanda (from *amare*)—someone loveable.

##### Infinitive Formation

You have already learned the present and perfect infinitives and participles. In this section, you’ll learn how to form the future as well.

Remember that only in the present do infinitives differ based on conjugation. Perfect and future infinitives are the same for all conjugation (1^st^ is given here as reference).

| Infinitives | Active | Passive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Present | vocāre   vidēre   regere   capere   audīre | vocārī   vidērī   regī   capī   audīrī |
| Perfect | vocāvisse | vocātus, -a, -um esse |
| Future | vocātūrus, -a, -um esse | vocātum īrī* |

To form the future infinitive, simply add the helping verb to the future participle. \*The future passive infinitive, which is formed by taking the 4^th^ principal part ending in –um (a form also called the **supine**) and and adding the helping verb *īrī*, is uncommon and does not need to be memorized.\*

The future infinitive of *sum* is *futurum esse*. This is often shortened into *fore*, which can stand for any form of the future infinitive (*futurum esse*, *futuram esse*, *futurōs esse*, etc.).

##### Gerund Formation

The gerund takes the same form as the gerundive, but it only exists as a neuter singular noun, and there is no nominative (since the infinitive takes its place).

| Gerund forms: | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | 3rd -io | 4th |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Genitive | vocandī | videndī | regendī | capiendī | audiendī |
| Dative | vocandō | videndō | regendō | capiendō | audiendō |
| Accusative | vocandum | videndum | regendum | capiendum | audiendum |
| Ablative | vocandō | videndō | regendō | capiendō | audiendō |

Use of Gerunds and Gerundives

One of the most common (and somewhat specialized) use is a pattern used for gerunds and gerundives (and although not covered here, in supines).

Pattern recognition—a verb of motion (*venīre, currere,* etc.), plus the preposition *ad*, plus an accusative gerund or gerundive shows purpose: *cucurrimus ad templum servāndum*: “we ran to protect the temple”

##### Gerundive as a replacement for the gerund

Since gerunds are verbal nouns, they can take direct objects (discimus legendō librōs = we learn by reading books; advēnimus ad capiendum urbes = we have come to capture the cities). This was form found in early Latin and in Late/Medieval Latin. Classical Latin, though, authors often replaced the gerund with a gerundive, then changed the direct object to match the case that the gerund would have had (note that this is an impossible construction in English). This seems complicated, but identifying and translating it is fairly intuitive after some practice.

Instead of *discimus legendō librōs*, Classical authors would write *discimus legendīs librīs*. Since nouns and adjectives must agree in case, number, and gender, the case is determined by gerund form (which is now a gerundive), while the gender and number are determined by the noun. The translation is literally “we learn by book readings,” but we translate it the same as we would a gerund: “we learn by reading books.”

##### Passive periphrastic

Sometimes the gerundive is combined with form of *sum*. This combination makes an even stronger sense of obligation. In this formation, the Ablative of Agent is replaced with a Dative of Agent (without a preposition).

spes habenda est = hope *must be* held.

deī colendī sunt = the gods *must be* worshipped.

littera scribenda est mihi = the letter *must be* written by me. (or) I must write the letter.

function normalizeGer(text) {
    return text
        .toLowerCase()
        .normalize("NFD")
        .replace(/[\u0300-\u036f]/g, "")
        .replace(/[.,!?;:'’"]/g, "")
        .replace(/\s+/g, " ")
        .trim();
}

    const possible =
        gerChangeQuestions.length +

            );

            q.answers.some(function(answer) {
                return student === normalizeGer(answer);

            document.getElementById(
