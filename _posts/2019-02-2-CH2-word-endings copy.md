### Principles of grammar

Today, words that change forms (he brings, he brought; child, children) are thought to be irregular, but most languages (including English), began with lots of different endings for all words. Speakers changed these endings according to the role each word pays in the sentence. Most modern languages instead use word order to express this information. This is called **inflection** (from the Latin word for *flex* or *bend*, because one can bend the words in different ways to mean something different).

Consider the phrase “The friends meet the players.”

You already read by patterns, but English patterns rely more on word order than Latin. We know who (friends) is doing something (drink) and what they are doing (coffee). We also know that the friends are mine (and not the coffee) and that the coffee is hot (and not necessarily my friends). In many ancient languages, though, speakers would express this information through word endings instead.

We could create a language system in which endings of words tell us what the word is doing, so if:

-   ! means “performs the action” (i.e., Subject)
-   \# means “receives the action” (i.e., Direct Object)

Then we could move the words around to emphasize different parts of the sentence:

-   Meet friends! players# “The friends meet the players.”
-   Friends! players# meet “The friends meet the players.”
-   Friends# players! meet “The players meet the friends.”
-   Players! friends# meet “The players meet the friends.”

We’ll see that the Romans used endings not only to show what a noun is doing in a sentence, but number (one friend or many friends), but what verbs are doing as well. **When** the friends and players meet, **if** they meet, and more. All of this is told with endings, so they are essential to memorize.

**The most important task you have in starting Latin is to identify this new pattern of endings instead of word order. This is like driving in a new country.** Lots of the rules are still the same, but there are important differences (which side of the road should you drive on?).

-   Latin uses inflections, but instead of exclamation marks or pound signs, the Romans just used different letters at the end of words.
-   For now, we will focus on 2 types of inflected words (nouns and verbs).

Transitive/Intransitive

In active voice sentences, words that perform the action are called **subjects**. Words that receive that action are called **direct objects**.

In her book *Word by Word*, Kory Stamper describes the sophisticated method by which editors of the *Merriam Webster Dictionary* determine how transitive a verb is. They have a notecard with the words “I'ma \_\_\_\_ yo ass!” written on it (with a hole cut out for the verb in question). If the editor puts the card over a verb and the sentence makes grammatical sense, then it’s transitive.

-   try with verbs like SNEEZE, MOVE, REALIZE, CALL, CONSIDER, SLEEP. Which ones are transitive?
-   Another simple way to test if a verb is transitive is to see if what? or whom? work with i.
    -   “they see” what? (a movie)
    -   “he knows” whom? (the plot)
    -   This test won’t work with “she stares” what?

### Principles of Latin

##### Verbs

There are (for now) 2 families (called **conjugations**) of verbs: a verbs and e verbs. There is not a significant difference to these verbs; they just belong to different families and have slight spelling variations.

For these verbs (and almost all verbs), t signifies “he/she/it \_\_\_\_\_s” and nt signifies “they \_\_\_\_\_”. Unlike English, Latin, like many other languages, does not require a named subject. When we translate, we should supply an appropriate: *vocant* = *they call*.

This form (called the **present tense**) can be translated in multiple ways in English. The most common are

-   simple present: *they call*
-   emphatic present: *they do call*
-   progressive present: *they are calling*

We’ll visit these concepts more in chapter 5, but for now just remember that they are all good ways to translate.

vocat/vocant: he/she/it calls; they call

videt/vident: he/she/it sees; they see

habet/habent: he/she/it has; they have

vocat/vocant: he/she/it calls; they call

movet/movent: he/she/it moves; they move

est/sunt: he/she/it is; they are (This is an irregular, but very common verb. It does not belong to any verb family, but it still uses the same endings.)

##### Nouns

There are several different families (called **declensions**) of nouns. These also have spelling variations that will be covered in depth later. The ending of a noun signifies its function in the sentence. In the following list, nouns ending in **m** (singular) or **s** (plural) are direct objects of sentences.

For now, any noun that doesn’t end in **m** or **s** are subjects.

pater videt pueru**m**: the father sees the boy

pater pueru**m** videt: the father sees the boy

videt pater pueru**m**: the father sees the boy

pater videt puer**os**: the father sees the boys

patrēs vident pueru**m**: **the fathers** see the boy

puerī patrem vident: **the boys** see the father

-   In the last two sentences, the subjects were plural.
-   Even if you don’t know all the singular or plural endings, you can tell its number by the verb ending (-t/-nt).

##### Vocabulary memorization

When you learn a noun, there are 4 essential elements of each word you need to memorize. You’ll learn more about the terms later:

| puer—_nominative_ _singular_ | puerī—_genitive_ _singular_ | masculine—_gender_ | boy—_definition_ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

1.  *Nominative* *singular*: this is essentially where you’d look in a dictionary to find the word. It is also the **subject** form.
2.  *Genitive* *singular*: this is more important than the first: it tells you the noun’s *stem* (the form on which all other versions of the word will be based) and its family (which determines how the word will look as a subject, direct object, etc.)
3.  *Gender*: all Latin nouns belong to one of 3 genders: masculine; feminine; neuter. You CANNOT determine a noun’s gender by its ending, definition, appearance, or anything else. It must be memorized.
4.  *Definition*: remember that words change meaning all the time, so even though an English word may look like its Latin root, it won’t always mean the same thing. *Manufacture* comes from the Latin words *manus, manūs (f) hand* and *faciō, facere, fēcī, factus: to make*, but it means the opposite of “to make by hand.”

##### Noun Forms

Remember that you’ll learn memorize two forms of each noun. The first (nominative) tells you the dictionary entry; the second (genitive) tells you the noun’s family and its stem.

-   When the second form ends with an -ae (see chapter 3), it belongs to the **first declension**
-   When the second form ends with an -ī, it belongs to the **second declension**
-   When the second form ends with an -is, it belongs to **the third declension**

To find the noun’s stem (you’ll put all the endings you learn in these chapters on the stem), remove the ending from the second form.

locī — -ī = *loc* (stem)

patris — -is = *patr* (stem)

### Pattern Recognition

When memorizing a word, it’s important to remember not just what it means in English, but what it does in a sentence. Pay close attention to if a noun is a subject or object, or singular or plural. Decide if a verb is transitive or intransitive before you move to the rest of the sentence. If you have an intransitive verb, you don’t need to look for a direct object.
