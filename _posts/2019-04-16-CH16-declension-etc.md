### Principles of Grammar

Even though the 4^th^ and 5^th^ declensions are less common, they do include some of the most important words (house, thing, day), and their endings should be memorized. The **Vocative** case, from *vocō*, *vocāre*, is used for direct address. The **Locative** case, from *locus, locī*, is used in special circumstances to show location. It is like the Ablative use of location, but only occurs with names of some places, especially cities (e.g., *Roma; Athenae*), and the nouns *domus*, *humus*, and *rus* (see vocabulary below). The formation for the locative varies, but it typically looks like the Genitive or Dative. The locative form will be given with each noun.

The final two tenses for you to learn are the pluperfect and future perfect. The **pluperfect** tense refers to an action that is already complete in the past and uses the English helping verb *had*. E.g., “He bought (perfect) the car after he *had saved* (pluperfect) all his money.” The **future perfect** expresses an action that will be complete in the future and uses the English helping verb *will have*. E.g., “He *will have saved* (future perfect) his money before he *will buy* (future) a car.”

### Principles of Latin

Words that take the Locative have another rule. They still use accusative to show motion toward and ablative to show motion away, but they do not use prepositions like *ad, ex,* or *de*. Thus the Romans would write *venimus ad urbem* (we are coming to the city), but *venimus Romam* (we are coming to Rome). This is similar in English with the noun domus (home). We say “we are coming home,” not “we are coming to home.”

The genitive singular ending—remember that this determines the declension of a word—for 4^th^ declension is –*ūs*. For 5^th^ declension the ending is –*ēī* or –*eī*. The difference between the two depends on whether the letter before the ending is a vowel or consonant, but you just need to remember that either is possible.

As with all verbs, the future perfect and pluperfect are based on the third principal part (active) and fourth principal part (passive) of the verb.

Cases and Declensions

Formation of the Locative: Remember that this is only for a limited group of rules (most importantly, names of places, and the nouns *domus, humus,* and *rus*). The formation of the locative is a little complex, but the pattern of the locative is straightforward. If you see a word that falls into a locative category and it has an ending that looks like the genitive or ablative of that case, it is most likely a locative. A complete list of locative endings is in the index.

Formation of the vocative: For most words, the vocative is the same as the nominative. There are two exceptions, both of which only occur in the 2^nd^ declension. If a noun ends in *–us* in the nominative, its vocative is –*e*. If a noun ends in –*ius*, its vocative is -*ī*. This occurs most often for proper names (Titus becomes *Tite* in the vocative).

Common examples:

-   For puella, puellae (f), the vocative forms are puella (singular) and puellae (plural)
-   For amicus, amicī (m), the vocative forms are amic**e** (singular) and amicī (plural)
-   For filius, filiī (m), the vocative forms are fil**ī** (singular) and filiī (plural)

4th declension (masculine and feminine): This declension is based on nouns ending in -u. It has masculine, feminine, and neuter nouns.

| **singular** | **plural** |
| --- | --- |
| manus | manūs |
| manūs | manuum |
| manuī | manibus |
| manum | manūs |
| manū | manibus |

4th Declension Neuter: There is a noticeable difference in 4^th^ declension neuter singulars, as all but the genitive have the same ending.

| **singular** | **plural** |
| --- | --- |
| genū | genua |
| genūs | genuum |
| genū | genibus |
| genū | genua |
| genū | genibus |

5th Declension: This declension is based on nouns ending in -e. It only has masculine and feminine nouns.

| **singular** | **plural** |
| --- | --- |
| rēs | rēs |
| reī | rērum |

| rem | rēs |

Perfect System Forms

Perfect Infinitives: In addition to the present active and present passive infinitives, Latin has perfect infinitives and future infinitives (chapter 17, below).

As with all Latin verbs, anything in the perfect active system comes from the 3^rd^ principal part. Although it technically means “to have \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed,” it is more often used in indirect statement (see exercises below). The perfect passive infinitive, like everything in the perfect passive system, comes from the fourth principal part. It too will most often occur in indirect statement, but can be translated individually as “to have been \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ed.”

To form the perfect active infinitive, take the 3^rd^ principal part of the verb (*vocāvī, vīdī, rēxī, cēpī, audīvī*), and remove the final *–ī*. Then, add the ending *–isse*. This works for all regular verbs.

1^st^ conjugation: *vocāv* + *isse* vocāvisse (to have called)

2^nd^ conjugation: *vīd* + *isse* vīdisse (to have seen)

3^rd^ conjugation: *rēx* + *isse* rēxisse (to have ruled)

3^rd^ –io conjugation: *cēp* + *isse* cēpisse (to have seized)

4^th^ conjugation: *audīv* + *isse* audīvisse (to have heard)

To form the perfect passive infinitive, simply add the infinitive of *sum* to the 4^th^ principal part. This should be intuitive, since you add an inflected form of *sum* to make the perfect passive indicative.

**\*Note:** like all perfect passive verbs, the gender and number of the infinitive can change to reflect the subject. Because you will most often see the perfect passive infinitive in indirect statement, you will often see it with accusative endings.

1^st^ conjugation: vocātus, -a, -um esse (to have been called)

2^nd^ conjugation: vīsus, -a, -um esse (to have been seen; to have seemed)

3^rd^ conjugation: rectus, -a, -um esse (to have been ruled)

3^rd^ –io conjugation: captus, -a, -um esse (to have been seized)

4^th^ conjugation: auditus, -a, -um esse (to have been heard)

##### Pluperfect and Future Perfect active:

For all verbs (all conjugations and irregular verbs), these forms are based on the perfect active stem (3^rd^ principal part, minus the *i*). To form the pluperfect, add –*era*\- and personal endings. To form the future perfect, add *–eri-* and personal endings. The first person singular ends simply in -*ero*

**Active:**

Pluperfect: Future perfect:

| vocāveram   (I had called) | vocāveramus   (we had called) | vocāverō   (I will have called) | vocāverimus   (we will have called) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| vocāveras   (you had called) | vocāveratis   (y’all had called) | vocāveris   (you will have called) | vocāveritis   (y’all will have called) |
| vocāverat   (he/she/it had called) | vocāverant   (they had called) | vocāverit   (he/she/it will have called) | vocāverint   (they will have called) |

**\*note** that the 3^rd^ person plural, future perfect ends in *int*. This is not a typical ending, but -*unt* cannot be used due to its use in the perfect tense.

**Passive:**

Pluperfect: Future perfect:

| vocātus, -a, -um eram   (I had been called) | vocātī, -ae, -a eramus   (we had been called) | vocātus, -a, -um erō   (I will have been called) | vocātī, -ae, -a erimus   (we will have been called) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| vocātus, -a, -um eras   (you had been called) | vocātī, -ae, -a eratis   (y’all had been called) | vocātus, -a, -um eris   (you will have been called) | vocātī, -ae, -a eritis   (y’all will have been called) |
| vocātus, -a, -um erat   (he/she/it had been called) | vocātī, -ae, -a erant   (they had been called) | vocātus, -a, -um erit   (he/she/it will have been called) | vocātī, -ae, -a erunt   (they will have been called) |

Use of infinitives in indirect statement: Although these infinitives can often appear as subjects or objects, their most common use is indirect statement. As you learned in Chapter 11, the tense of an infinitive is relative to the tense of the head verb in an indirect statement. A perfect infinitive shows action *prior to* the head verb.

Examples:

Head Verb Infinitive Translation

*Caesar dicit eōs venīre* present present Caesar says that they are coming

*Caesar dīxit eōs venīre* perfect present Caesar said that they were coming

*Caesar dicit eōs vēnisse* present perfect Caesar says that they came

*Caesar dīxit eōs vēnisse* perfect perfect Caesar said that they had come
