### Principles of Grammar

Attributes of verbs: **Tense**. Here is where English and Latin are slightly different. In Latin (and in some degree English), tense involves two elements: time and **aspect**. The time is self-explanatory; it refers to when the action takes place.  
Aspect, which is often conflated with time, refers to the completion or duration of an action. English has more aspects than Latin, so it can sometimes be difficult to translate in the same sense. You’ve seen that in English one can say “I think,” “I am thinking,” and “I do think.” All of these are happening in the present, but they have slightly different meanings. Similarly, one can say: “I was thinking,” “I did think,” “I thought,” “I used to think,” and even “I have been thinking.” All the verbs happened in the past, but they all have different aspects.  
Latin essentially has two aspects. For now, we’ll call them *simple* and *finished*.

-   Simple tenses
    -   **present** – ongoing (I work, I do work, I am working)
    -   **imperfect** – past ongoing (I was working, I used to work, I kept working)
    -   **future** – later action (I will work) \*to be learned in chapter 15
-   Finished tenses
    -   **perfect** – complete, either in past or present\* (I worked, I have worked, I did work)
    -   **pluperfect** – prior complete action (I had worked): to be learned in chapter 15
    -   **future** **perfect** – subsequent complete action (I will have worked): to be learned in chapter 15

\*Although it seems counterintuitive to describe a present complete action, think about the phrase “I have cleaned all day, and now you’re making a mess.” Even though the action (*I have cleaned*) is complete, the importance of the action is in the present.

### Principles of Latin

##### Imperfect Tense

In the simple tenses, changes are made by adding a tense marker between the verb and its ending. For the most part, the word is formed in this way:

VERB STEM + tense marker + personal endings

For 1st and 2nd^[\[2\]](#footnote-2)^ conjugation verbs, it looks like this:

1^st^ conjugation:

VOCĀ + -ba- + m = vocābam

VOCĀ + -bā- + s = vocābās

VOCĀ + -ba- + t = vocābat

VOCĀ + -bā- + mus = vocābāmus

VOCĀ + -bā- + tis = vocābātis

VOCĀ + -ba- + nt = vocābant

2^nd^ conjugation:

VIDĒ + -ba- + m = vidēbam

VIDĒ + -ba- + s = vidēbās

VIDĒ + -ba- + t = vidēbat

VIDĒ + -ba- + mus = vidēbāmus

VIDĒ + -ba- + tis = vidēbātis

VIDĒ + -ba- + nt = vidēbant

3^rd^ and 4^th^ conjugation are slightly different.

-   For 3^rd^, the thematic vowel (short *e*) is lengthened before adding the endings. This is unlike the formation of the present tense, when the vowel was shortened to an *i*.
-   For 4^th^, a long *e* is added, but the thematic *i* remains (all forms of the 4^th^ conjugation must have an *i* in the simple tenses (present, imperfect, and future).

3^rd^ conjugation:

REGĒ + -ba- + m = regēbam

REGĒ + -ba- + s = regēbās

REGĒ + -ba- + t = regēbat

REGĒ + -ba- + mus = regēbāmus

REGĒ + -ba- + tis = regēbātis

REGĒ + -ba- + nt = regēbant

4^th^ conjugation

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + m = audiēbam

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + s = audiēbās

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + t = audiēbat

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + mus = audiēbāmus

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + tis = audiēbātis

AUDI + Ē + -ba- + nt = audiēbant

##### Perfect Tense

The perfect tense has slightly different personal endings, but the good news is that they apply to every single verb in Latin, even the most irregular ones.

To form the perfect tense, take the 3rd principal part and remove the final ī. This leaves you with the **perfect active stem**. To this stem, simply add the personal endings:

| -ī | -īmus |
| --- | --- |
| -istī | -istis |
| -it | \-ērunt/ēre[\[3\]](#footnote-3) |

So no matter what conjugation the verb is, it will adopt this paradigm:

| vocav**ī** | vocav**īmus** |
| --- | --- |
| vocav**istī** | vocav**istis** |
| vocav**it** | vocav**ērun**t / vocav**ēre** |

Depending on the aspect you want to get across in English, you can translate the perfect tense in a variety of ways: they called, they did call, they have called.

##### Forms of Sum, esse, fuī

The linking verb (sum, esse, fui, futurus) can also be conjugated in the present, imperfect, and perfect. These forms appear everywhere and are best memorized. They do follow the typical pattern of personal endings but seem slightly irregular. For those interested, this type of irregular verb is formed through **suppletion**, and it occurs in most languages (including English).

| Present |  | Imperfect |  | Perfect |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| sum | sumus | eram | eramus | fuī | fuimus |
| es | estis | eras | eratis | fuistī | fuistis |
| est | sunt | erat | erant | fuit | fuērunt/ēre |

This is another area in which Latin and English do not match up as well. Both the imperfect and perfect are usually best translated as “they were,” but you can distinguish between the imperfect (“they used to be”) and perfect (“they have been”) if necessary.
